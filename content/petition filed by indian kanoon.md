**SYNOPSIS**

The present Letters Patent Appeal challenges the common judgment dated 29.05.2026 of the Ld. Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court in *DG v. Union of India & Ors., W.P.(C) NO.* *9824/2023* and 37 other connected writ petitions ("Impugned Judgment"), whereby the Ld. Single Judge has made a broad direction to the present Appellant to "de-index, and disable, across all platforms and domains, name-based search functionality in respect of the judgments/orders/news articles complained of in the petition" (Impugned Judgment, [246]), without fully appreciating the prejudice caused to the right to information and the goals of open justice.

In brief, the Appellant challenges the Impugned Judgment on the following grounds:

a. The Impugned Judgment has misconstrued the decision in K.S. Puttaswamy (Privacy- *9J.) v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1,* ***("Puttaswamy I"),*** wherein this Hon'ble Court recognized the right to privacy as an intrinsic part of the right to life and liberty under Article 21 of the Constitution, but did not establish a "right to be forgotten" in the Indian context. The mere fact that there was a brief discussion by way of obiter in *Puttaswamy* *I* about the right to be forgotten is insufficient to be the legal basis for extending a "right to be forgotten" to individual litigants. Thus, *Puttaswamy I* does not provide an unqualified right to be forgotten from historical or judicial records.

b. This Hon'ble Court in Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P., (2023) 4 SCC 1, [49-50], has noted that there can be no further restrictions other than those prescribed in Article 19(2) on freedom of speech and expression. Under the guise of invoking a fundamental right, or two competing fundamental rights, litigants cannot seek to extend beyond the kind of legitimate aims identified under Article 19(2) of the Constitution of India.

c. The Impugned Judgment has framed an arbitrary standard for de-indexing and disabling name-based searches, namely, the information must be "no longer relevant" or "does not serve a legitimate public purpose" (Impugned Judgment, [141]). Litigants claiming

'privacy' from search engines and legal databases have to prove that the information contained therein is no longer relevant and serves no legitimate public purpose. However, the factors of "relevance" and "legitimate public purpose" are arbitrary, broad, and unclear. Without a clear and exhaustive list of the type of cases where names ought to be masked as a matter of practice, different judges may reach different conclusions, which could lead to arbitrary censorship and restraint on the retrievability or searchability of court records.

d. The Impugned Judgment directed global de-indexing in the underlying plea in W.P.(C) No. 9824/2023, without an evidentiary fact-finding assessment of harm, and merely on bare assertion of harm (Impugned Judgment, [196]). The Ld. Single Judge observes that *"masking of subordinate court records is more appropriately left to the originating* *court"* (Impugned Judgment, [219]), and that the petitioners are at liberty to seek masking of their names and personal identifiers from the publicly accessible digital versions of judicial records by moving an application before the concerned Court that rendered the original order or judgment (Impugned Judgment, [222]). However, the Ld Single Judge ought to have also left declaratory reliefs as to whether each case meets the evidentiary threshold for harm in respect of masking of names to the determination of the concerned courts.

e. The Impugned Judgment's directions to censure particular names from search results ought to be first addressed at the point of origin (i.e. court registries) upon consideration of relevant harms that might ensue to persons involved or mentioned in the court orders or judgments, prior to enforcing it upon private service providers like the present Appellant.

f. The Impugned Judgment has arbitrarily and in violation of the right to equality enshrined under Article 14 of the Constitution made directions for disabling namebased search functionality only against one legal database, namely, the present Appellant, even though there are several other case management platforms available

online for cataloguing and searching judgments/orders of various Indian courts and tribunals. The direction of the Ld Single Judge creates a classification discriminating against the Appellant.

g. The Impugned Judgement has unreasonably restricted the right to freedom of trade, occupation and profession of the Petitioner guaranteed by Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution. Users most commonly enter party names to find and apply cases. Such users include litigants, students, researchers, lawyers, and even judges themselves. If individual litigants are given the impetus to seek deletion of their names, i.e., redaction of case names from the Petitioner's database, it has a direct adverse impact on the usability and utility of the Petitioner's legal search engine, thereby impairing the Petitioner's freedom to carry out its business, guaranteed by Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution.

h. The Impugned Judgment fails to recognise that the lives of court cases are recorded in mediums beyond legal databases, insofar as they find mention in digital news media, social media posts, and journalistic platforms. The Ld Single Judge ought to have framed the reliefs in such a manner that they address the issue at the point of origin (court registries must be directed to mask or redact names of parties who are entitled to such protection based on evidence of harm), and the private parties who must give effect to the directions for masking or redaction. This requires clearer rules/legislation, data fiduciaries and publishers who ought to respect the masking/redaction directions as a responsibility.

i. The Impugned Judgment's attempt to distinguish itself from a case pending before the Hon'ble Supreme Court, namely, *Ikanoon Software Development Pvt. Ltd. v. Karthick* *Theodore (SLP(C) No. 15311 of 2024)* does not withstand legal scrutiny as Karthick Theodore had approached the Madras High Court only seeking to redact his name and other identifying details from the judgment dated 30.04.2014 in Crl.A (MD) No. 321/2011. This is similar to the reliefs sought by the writ petitions considered by the

Ld Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court in the Impugned Judgment. However, the Hon'ble Division Bench of the Madras High Court, on 27.02.2024 in W.A. (MD) No. 1901 of 2021 ordered the Petitioner to remove the entire judgment, thereby creating an incorrect precedent with significant implications for the Petitioner's freedom of speech and expression. Resultantly, one of the questions of law was framed to question whether a court can direct a legal database to take down a judgment in its entirety. This does not change the fact that the reliefs originally sought by Karthick Theodore were similar to the ones considered by the Ld Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court in the Impugned Judgment.

In view of the submissions made hereinabove, it is most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court be pleased to set aside the Impugned Judgment dated 29.05.2026 passed by the Ld. Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court. **LIST OF DATES**

#### GROUNDS

A. THAT the Impugned Judgment cast a broad and impractical direction to "deindex, and disable, across all platforms and domains, name-based search functionality in respect of the judgments/orders/news articles complained of in the petition" based on a purported 'right to be forgotten' (Impugned Judgment, [246]).

B. THAT the Impugned Judgment has misconstrued the decision in K.S. *Puttaswamy (Privacy-9J.) v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1,* ***("Puttaswamy*** ***I"),*** wherein this Hon'ble Court recognized the right to privacy as an intrinsic part of the right to life and liberty under Article 21 of the Constitution, but did not establish a "right to be forgotten" in the Indian context. The "right to be forgotten" was discussed by Justice Kaul in *Puttaswamy I, [632-636],* when he noted the existence of a similar right in Article 17 (right of erasure) in EU's General Data Protection Regulation. In the context of Indian law, Justice Kaul did not affirmatively decide the question of the existence of the right to be forgotten. Rather, he observed that if India "were to recognise a similar right", such a right operates in a manner such that "an individual who is no longer desirous of his personal data to be processed or stored, should be able to remove it from the system where the personal data/information is no longer necessary, relevant, or is incorrect and serves no legitimate interest". Justice Kaul in *Puttaswamy I, [636]* also expressly recognised that any exercise of the right to be forgotten or right of erasure must be subject to reasonable limitations, including *(a)* other fundamental rights such as freedom of speech

and expression under Article 19(1)(a), *(b)* compliance with legal obligations, *(c)* public interest, *(d)* public health, *(e)* archiving, *(f)* scientific, historical, or statistical research, and *(g)* the defence of legal claims. These limitations reflect the principle that judicial and public records serve a continuing public function and cannot be obliterated merely based on individual request. The mere fact that there was a brief discussion by way of obiter in *Puttaswamy I* about the right to be forgotten is insufficient to be the legal basis for extending a "right to be forgotten" to individual litigants. Thus, *Puttaswamy I* does not provide an unqualified right to be forgotten from historical or judicial records.

C. THAT the Hon'ble Supreme Court, in R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N., (1994) 6 *SCC 632, [26],* recognized that the right to privacy is implicit in the right to life and liberty guaranteed by Article 21 of the Constitution, but does not extend to protect an individual from publications of public records, including court records.

D. THAT court records are public documents whose reproduction or publication cannot be prohibited except for well-curated exceptions carved out through constitutional scrutiny and legislative mandate to meet the ends of justice, *inter alia,*

    a. cases of rape and other sexual offences, where the victims' identities are protected, as held in *Nipun Saxena v. Union of India,* (2019) 2 SCC 703, [50-51];

    b. cases involving statutory prohibitions on disclosure, such as Section 22 of Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, Section 228-A of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, Sections 72 and 73 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, Section 327(3) of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (corresponding to

Section 366 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023), and Section 23 of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012;

    c. miscellaneous legal proceedings incidental to cases under (a) and (b), including appeals and contempt petitions; and

    d. cases where the High Court, in exercise of its powers under Article 226 of the Constitution, issues postponement or redaction orders, as recognised in *Sahara India Real Estate Corp. Ltd. v. SEBI, (2012) 10* *SCC 603, [36-50].*

E. THAT the mere desire of an individual to erase the record of their litigation history cannot be elevated to a ground of restriction under Article 19(2). The relief to delete court records cannot be granted based on any of the legitimate aims identified in Article 19(2), namely, "sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with Foreign States, public order, decency or morality or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence". This Hon'ble Court in *Kaushal Kishor v. State of* *U.P., (2023) 4 SCC 1,* [49-50], has noted that there can be no further restrictions other than those prescribed in Article 19(2) on freedom of speech and expression. Under the guise of invoking a fundamental right, or two competing fundamental rights, litigants cannot seek to extend beyond the kind of legitimate aims identified under Article 19(2) of the Constitution of India.

F. THAT the Impugned Judgment erroneously gives effect to the reliefs of deindexing and disabling name-based searches, and reads them as components of a 'right to be forgotten' (Impugned Judgment, [141]). These reliefs are framed as particular reliefs to which individuals must be entitled to as part of

the purported "right to be forgotten" and the right to informational privacy under Article 21 of the Constitution of India.

G. THAT the Impugned Judgment has framed an arbitrary standard for deindexing and disabling name-based searches, namely, the information must be "no longer relevant" or "does not serve a legitimate public purpose" (Impugned Judgment, [141]). Litigants claiming 'privacy' from search engines and legal databases have to prove that the information contained therein, is no longer relevant and serves no legitimate public purpose. However, the factors of "relevance" and "legitimate public purpose" are arbitrary, broad, and unclear. If this Hon'ble Court does not determine an exhaustive list of cases in which litigants can seek de-indexing and/or disabling of name-based searches for court records in a particular case, different judges may reach different conclusions, which could lead to arbitrary censorship and restraint on retrievability or searchability of court record.

H. THAT the global de-indexing direction given in the underlying plea in W.P.(C) No. 9824/2023, ought to have been based on an evidentiary factfinding assessment of harm, and not merely a prima facie case of supposed harm (Impugned Judgment, [196]). One of the inclusive set of legal parameters that the Ld Single Judge indicated in the Impugned Judgment, [196], is the *"the impact upon dignity and autonomy, including reputational* *harm, stigma, or disproportionate interference with the individual's ability to* *lead a dignified life, weighed against any legitimate public welfare served by* *disclosure".* Even though the Ld. Single Judge observes that *"masking of* *subordinate court records is more appropriately left to the originating court"* (Impugned Judgment, [219]), and that the petitioners are at liberty to seek masking of their names and personal identifiers from the publicly accessible

digital versions of judicial records by moving an application before the concerned Court that rendered the original order or judgment (Impugned Judgment, [222]), it does not leave the determination of declaratory reliefs as to whether masking is needed in the individual cases to the determination of the concerned courts.

I. THAT the Impugned Judgment has expanded the scope for arbitrary censorship by instituting a standard of "relevance" and "legitimate public purpose", and granted reliefs of de-indexation and disabling of name-based searches based on bare assertions of harm in 36 of the 38 writ petitions.

J. THAT the order to censure particular names from search results ought to be first addressed at the point of origin (i.e. court registries) upon consideration of relevant harms that might ensue to persons involved or mentioned in the court orders or judgments, prior to enforcing it upon private service providers like the present Appellant.

K. THAT the Impugned Judgment has arbitrarily and in violation of the right to equality enshrined under Article 14 of the Constitution made directions for disabling name-based search functionality only against one legal database, namely, the present Appellant. The Appellant is one of many case management platforms available online for cataloguing and searching judgments/orders of various Indian courts and tribunals. Most case management platforms are restricted by paywalls, requiring users to purchase a subscription or membership to access the records through their respective search engines. Therefore, directing only the Appellant, and not any other case management platform, to remove particular judgments or orders from its database, creates a classification discriminating against the Appellant. This

Hon'ble Court has settled the position that, for a classification made by any law to pass the anvil of Article 14, it must be reasonable. A classification is reasonable if it satisfies the two-pronged test laid down in *Amalgamated Tea* *Estates Co. Ltd. v. State of Kerala, (1974) 4 SCC 415, [8],* namely, (i) the classification should be based on an intelligible differential, and (2) the differentia should bear a rational relation to the purpose of the legislation. There is no intelligible differentia in the direction made by the Impugned Judgment, as the Appellant has been singled out from a host of paid and unpaid case management platforms available online, and ordered to delete the record of a particular judgement from its database. The Impugned Judgement, thus, falls on the first limb of unreasonable classification itself. In any case, even if an intelligible differentia were to be made out, there is no rational nexus between making such a classification among different case management platforms and the purported aim of the measure, i.e., privacy of the individual litigant; the court records in question being available on any platform or software create digital footprint for the litigant, that can be claimed to mar his right to be forgotten, thereby rendering it superfluous to order only a single such platform to delete the concerned record.

L. THAT the Impugned Judgement has unreasonably restricted the right to freedom of trade, occupation and profession of the Petitioner guaranteed by Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution. The Petitioner primarily uses case names/cause titles for making its considerable repository of court decisions accessible via a free search engine. Thereby, users most commonly enter party names to find and apply cases. Such users include litigants, students, researchers, lawyers, and even judges themselves. If individual litigants are given the impetus to seek deletion of their names, i.e., redaction of case names

from the Petitioner's database, it has a direct adverse impact on the usability and utility of the Petitioner's legal search engine, thereby impairing the Petitioner's freedom to carry out its business, guaranteed by Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution. Such an impairment is valid and justifiable only if it is effectuated by State action for one of the enumerated purposes under Article 19(6) and in the manner prescribed therein, not by judicial action. This test has developed further across a catena of judgments by this Hon'ble Court, including, notably, *Mohd. Faruk v. State of M.P., (1969) 1 SCC 853, [10].* Therefore, it is submitted that there is an element of proportionality in adjudging whether a restriction in question is reasonable under the jurisprudence governing Article 19(1)(g) or not. The restriction being imposed on the Petitioner's Article 19(1)(g) freedoms is neither consonant with the permissible grounds and procedure delineated in Article 19(6), nor does it meet the test of proportionality posited in the above-cited decision of the Hon'ble Supreme Court.

M.THAT the principle of double proportionality, as applied by the Hon'ble Supreme Court in *Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India,* *(2024) 5 SCC 1,* ("Electoral Bonds" case) applies where legislation restricts one fundamental right on the purported ground of protecting a countervailing right. This Hon'ble Court must apply the proportionality test twice — once for the right being restricted and once for the right being protected. In *Electoral Bonds* (supra), the Hon'ble Supreme Court struck down the scheme because the right to know was entirely effaced in an attempt to protect donors' privacy, when less restrictive alternatives existed. The same logic applies here with equal force. The right to information has been entirely subordinated to an undefined and unlimited "right to be forgotten" as a limb of the right to privacy, when less restrictive alternatives were present.

N. THAT the Impugned Judgment fails to consider that private legal databases like the present Appellant exist because most official court websites are poor, slow, and unreliable. Several court websites and tribunal/consumer forum websites do not have advanced free-search options and are riddled with idiosyncratic querying limitations: case-type abbreviations vary between different court registries, login sessions expire, captchas interrupt access, inconsistent formats resist searchability and portability of judgments. Legal database platforms like the present Appellant enhance the ability of lawyers to undertake legal research, ordinary citizens to understand and engage with law pronounced by courts and enhance democratic participation, and bridge access and affordability gaps that widen the bridge between lawyers who can access paid subscription based legal databases and those who cannot afford to pay for such services. The Appellant's legal database is also used for identifying persons who act as witnesses in several cases at the district court level.

O. THAT the Impugned Judgment fails to recognise that the lives of court cases are recorded in mediums beyond legal databases, insofar as they find mention in digital news media, social media posts, and journalistic platforms. While the intention is to rehabilitate and enable people to move past their entanglements with the legal system, the proposed measure is not cognisant of real constraints that prevent the person's name from being completely *disconnected* from the case on search engines. The Ld Single Judge ought to have framed the reliefs in such a manner that they address the issue at the point of origin (court registries must be directed to mask or redact names of

parties who are entitled to such protection based on evidence of harm), and the private parties who must give effect to the directions for masking or redaction. This requires clearer rules/legislation, data fiduciaries and publishers who ought to respect the masking/redaction directions as a responsibility.

P. THAT the Impugned Judgment's attempt to distinguish itself from a case pending before the Hon'ble Supreme Court, namely, *Ikanoon Software* *Development Pvt. Ltd. v. Karthick Theodore, SLP(C) No. 15311 of 2024* does not withstand legal scrutiny as Karthick Theodore had approached the Madras High Court only seeking to redact his name and other identifying details from the judgment dated 30.04.2014 in Crl.A (MD) No. 321/2011. This is similar to the reliefs sought by the writ petitions considered by the Ld Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court in the Impugned Judgment. However, the Hon'ble Division Bench of the Madras High Court, on 27.02.2024 in W.A. (MD) No. 1901 of 2021 ordered the Petitioner to remove the entire judgment, thereby creating an incorrect precedent with significant implications for the Petitioner's freedom of speech and expression. Resultantly, one of the questions of law was framed to question whether a court can direct a legal database to take down a judgment in its entirety. This does not change the fact that the reliefs originally sought by Karthick Theodore were similar to the ones considered by the Ld Single Judge of this Hon'ble Court in the Impugned Judgment.

Q. THAT this Hon'ble Court is duty-bound to step in when there is a legislative vacuum and fundamental rights are at stake (Impugned Judgment, [166]) is inaccurate. This Hon'ble Court does not have the powers of the nature contemplated by Article 32 read with Article 142 of the Constitution of India

to grant reliefs on the basis of new 'rights' that are not contemplated by legislation or existing precedent.
