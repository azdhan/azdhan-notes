# Who Regulates the Regulators?

There was an internet shutdown in Dehradun — reason being to prevent violence following the killing of a BJP functionary. And this is exactly the problem. A state's failure to maintain law and order shouldn't become an excuse for wide-ranging restrictions on internet access. **Access to the internet should be fundamental.** You're not blocking a weapon — you're blocking a medium. The more honest argument, if there is one, is about the velocity at which content travels from one person to many. The scale and nature of social media does complicate how you think about regulation. But a blanket ban is still not the answer.

**The Australian e-Safety Report:** The e-safety commissioner's report from Australia says 60% of parents felt children under 16 showed positive behavioural changes after restricted social media access. I'm skeptical — the YouGov survey may have been conducted on behalf of the same government making the policy. Prateek's article on Asian Dispatch — _Locking Out: Why Blanket Social Media Bans Won't Protect Children_ — is worth reading here. Lots of hyperlinked references inside.

**Internet as public commons:** Nikhil Pahwa's core argument, which I find genuinely compelling, is that **the internet is not a thing — it's a global public commons**. Nobody owns the internet because its infrastructure runs on spectrum, and we elect governments to distribute that spectrum to private players, who then enable ordinary users to communicate and connect. So the internet is, at root, a public resource. This is also, I think, the foundational argument for **net neutrality** — the commons shouldn't favour one party over another.

**The concentration problem:** The other big concern Nikhil raises is the **concentration of power** into a few platforms. If you're a content creator, you're dependent on Meta or YouTube. If you're in e-commerce, you're dependent on Amazon. He uses the analogy of these platforms as _countries_ — Meta, Amazon, YouTube as nation-states, and Zuckerberg, Bezos, Musk as heads of state. How these platforms are designed has real consequences for real nations. Even the former media advisor to the PM noted that when big tech figures come to India, every politician wants to rub shoulders with them. It's geopolitics at this point.

> Who is regulating who is the core friction here.

**The disappearing URL:** Something Nikhil flagged that I want to think about more — **applications are replacing the browser as the primary interface for the internet**. URLs are disappearing from what users actually see. His argument is that the browser used to _be_ the internet; now it's just another app on your phone. One concrete implication I can think of: when governments want platforms to take down content, the law asks for a specific URL. If URLs are disappearing from the user-facing layer, what does that process even look like going forward? I don't have a clean answer here.

**When TRAI got regulated by the App Store:** An illustrative moment from the podcast — TRAI tried to launch an anti-spam app, and Apple's App Store blocked it because it was asking for device-level permissions that would violate user privacy. Which raises an obvious question: a regulator trying to do its job is being blocked by a platform that is itself supposed to be regulated by the government. But I'd also note — the government has its own record here. _Sanchar Saathi_ asked for pretty extensive device access. So the privacy argument cuts in multiple directions.

**Algorithm neutrality:** One of Nikhil's key asks is **neutrality of algorithms** — reducing hyper-personalised targeting. The user's right to choose, accept, or deny. That's the principle. On the **HPCL/Amazon pricing** situation — Amazon was dropping prices on products without asking publishers or sellers first, which HPCL apparently objected to. The platform's argument would be: price drops increase volume, so everyone wins. But can or should an end consumer be served at the expense of a seller's right to price their own product? That question I'd want to sit with longer.

**Intermediary liability and Bajaj v. porn case:** I need to read the Bajaj case where the court held a platform liable for user-uploaded pornographic content — this is apparently the origin of intermediary liability in India. Where did it start, where is it now, where is it going? Worth a deep dive.

**On free speech:** Amit Varma seems to be a free speech absolutist. I want to be one too, but I want to understand the arguments before I start advocating. The key question in free speech is: not just where do we draw the line, but _should_ a line even exist? Their argument is that **when there is a clear and present danger** — when speech becomes an instrument of incitement and violence — it's no longer a free speech issue. You're not being silenced; your speech is being treated as a tool for harm. I need to think more about whether that framing holds.

**Surveillance as business model:** The broader arc of how we got here: user profiling began because advertisers wanted to eliminate the 50% of their audience they weren't reaching — stop wasting money on people who don't care. Once that infrastructure existed, it got absorbed into state apparatus. Now **surveillance and user profiling is a core function of running a state**. Someone named **Bruce Schneier** (I think — the name was unclear in my notes) apparently argued that the harms of large-scale data collection are greater than any utility it provides. His position: even if there's a case for collection, the downside outweighs the benefit.

**MediaNama's philosophy:** Open, fair, and competitive internet. Nikhil still wants competition — even now, while arguments are going on about the US government restricting Anthropic from distributing the Mythos model internationally. He made the point that depending on companies like _Sarvam_ in India will also be a problem if there's no diversification. Concentration is concentration, regardless of nationality.

**Article 66A:** Referenced in passing — I don't remember the exact context. I think Nikhil linked it to his early digital activism, before the Latvian tragedy (note: I think this might be a transcription error — possibly referring to _Laxmi Nagar_ or another incident — flagging this as uncertain). His online activism started with **Article 66A** of the IT Act.

**The 2006 blog ban:** Government of India blocked all blogs — WordPress, Typepad, Blogspot — because someone posted something objectionable on one of them, and the government's response was to ban the entire platform. That's where his digital activism started. And then under **Section 79** of the IT Act, there were cases asking whether a blog could be held liable for comments made on it. If that had gone the other way, comments sections would have died in India.

_Random footnote: the IT Act came in 2008 but the rules came in 2011._

**Rahul Gandhi and net neutrality:** At some point Rahul Gandhi tried to politicize the net neutrality debate. Nikhil was against that then and still is. His line: focus on issues, not politics or optics.

**On the centralisation of apps vs. the web:** The phone-as-infrastructure argument. Nikhil's point is that phones don't have the same capacity as the web. On a browser, you access multiple sites without downloading them. On a phone, apps have to be persistent — they require space, RAM, processor power. That's why phones are getting more powerful (there was a video comparing a OnePlus Pad's Snapdragon chip to a laptop processor — it won). But apps also work offline and have better UI. So it's not a clean win either way. Still, the direction of travel matters.
