# What young people have to say about social media ban and restrictions

Note: The discussion was held under the Chatham House Rule. All quotes have been edited for clarity, brevity and anonymity.

"You take away my Instagram. I will use Gmail, right? You take away my Gmail, I'll make reels on YouTube that only my friends can see. And, then if you take that away too, am I supposed to get a pigeon? Like, we have to find avenues to connect with young people. It isn't as simple as writing letters or, you know, sending a Gmail. Instagram and Reddit and I think Tumblr, they're one of the few platforms that allow young people to stay connected on their own terms," said a Northeastern female student who had just passed out of 12th grade. She made this statement in the context of her belonging to a Fauzi (Indian Army) family, and therefore being subjected to constant transfers every two years.

The young female said this during MediaNama’s roundtable discussion on Age Verification and Restricting Social Media for Children on July 31st in New Delhi. Here are a few key statements from the roundtable discussion on banning or restricting social media for young children under 18:

Banning/restricting social media will isolate underprivileged: "When we're talking about people from isolated or underspoken and underprivileged communities, and when we talk about restricting social media, we're talking about isolating them from large areas of knowledge and information," said a 17-year-old YLAC member

Social media restrctrions will also restrict social work that young people want to do: "When we're talking about restricting access to the internet, one thing that sets a precedent is a lot of these initiatives that children will lead, because children want to lead, right? We all see problems around us every day, and we have the capacity to solve it in our own way, right? If we restrict access to social media, how do you propose children do that?" said another 17-year-old YLAC member who was involved in social work, setting up a physical library of 5000 books. She said she got to know about this incubation program via Instagram. 

Children are smarter than adults assume they are and they can bypass the so-called safeguarding systems: "My cousin who is 13 today, she's bypassed Roblox's facial recognition by pulling up a stock picture on her phone. So when we're talking about age estimations using these probabilistic techniques, children are smarter than you assume they are," she further added. "So when we're talking about age estimations, we also need to talk about the fact that kids are, young people are smarter than you give them the credit for, and we're very, very good at finding alternate avenues," she said earlier in the discussion.

Simple mathematical questions used for age determination can also be bypassed: "There's another app called Toca Boca, which is again a game. I talked to my cousin before I came here to get to know what apps she uses. But basically, instead of an age estimation, you know, because it's really easy to lie, right? I can say I was born in 1977. Do I look like I'm born in 1977? So what they do is they give you a mathematical equation. Which is really scary for people who are afraid of maths, but then like, the equation that they give you is fairly easy for anyone older than the age of 13 to say. So it'll be something like 15 divided by 3. All of us will be able to say 5, right? But the six-year-old sitting there will be like, 'What is this sign with two dots here?' So I think even techniques like that are now easy to bypass because she pulls up a calculator," she said. 

Online discussions strengthen the courage to disagree and speak out:  "The only reason I can be here today is because I've spoken to people on the internet who've questioned my views, right? It has also given me the courage to talk to people face to face and disagree. I think, uh, my parents like to keep me in a room when their friends come because whenever they say something I don't disagree with I'm not the type to stay silent.""

Internet gives a space where the opinions of the people are valued more than for their appearance: "You fall in love with the person's personality and their ideas before looking at them for who they are, before the superficial metrics. The internet affords you that place for your opinions. People are judging you solely for what you say and not what you look like, not where you belong from, not where you come from. And I think that's something that we have to keep in mind for young people on the internet. We're all looking for belonging at the end of the day."

Internet can enable a safe space for the discussions that the offline world might not be ready for, like sexuality or identity: "I think a lot depends on the context you live in. Today, as someone living in urban Delhi, I can talk about most things with my offline peer groups. But when it comes to identity—and when the people around you still have a lot to unlearn, especially about identity and what you believe defines who you are—it becomes much easier to turn to online platforms where people understand your struggle. And especially when we're talking about things our parents wouldn't be comfortable discussing, we need somewhere to go and work through them on our own first. A lot of understanding who you are comes from talking with others, voicing your opinions, reflecting on your lived experiences, and comparing them to what others have gone through. If we didn't have that online space to turn to—and these are always last-resort alternatives, right? Like they mentioned, if we could get this understanding offline, we wouldn't seek it online. But when it comes to questions of identity, when your immediate family and the people around you won't understand, then the internet is where you'll go," said another female student.

"I think it's also simple topics like, someone who doesn't know what DU is, or someone who has never applied abroad, or someone who's never applied to Hong Kong University but wants to, and no one around them has either. They will turn to the internet, they will go to Reddit, and they will go to the subreddit Indians Abroad, and they will ask questions there. And then, when it comes to more serious issues, whether it's about your queer identity or political identity, what you choose to believe in, you would want to hash it out in a place that is more understanding of your views first. And I think the internet affords you that privilege," added another female young person.
