# Delhi HC summons Abhijit Iyer-Mitra, The Pamphlet in an alleged doxxing campaign

  

The Delhi High Court issued summons to political commentator Abhijit Iyer-Mitra, media platform The Pamphlet, legal news outlet Law Beat and two social media accounts in a lawsuit filed by Cockroach Janta Party’s (CJP) official spokesperson, Saurav Das over online doxing that led to public disclosure of his private home address. TThis was reported by [Bar and Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/news/litigation/delhi-hc-issues-summons-to-abhijit-iyer-mitra-the-pamphlet-x-others-on-suit-by-cjps-saurav-das-over-disclosure-of-his-address) first. 

  

Justice Sachin Datta also issued notice to the defendants on Das' plea for an interim injunction and posted the case for further hearing Sept. 14. The defendants have 10 days to file replies.

  

In his plea witht he HC, the CJP spokesperson sought more than Rs. 2 crore in damages and a permanent injunction barring the involved parties from publishing, circulating or disclosing his residential address. He has also asked the court to direct X and Google to take down the content and prevent its further dissemination.

  

Posts already taken down from X: During the court proceedings, X's advocate said that the posts related to Das’s addresses were already removed as they were violating X’s own terms. 

  

According to the suit, representatives of The Pamphlet trespassed into the shared residential premises in south Delhi's Greater Kailash-I neighborhood on August 5, recorded its interiors and posted the footage on X. The video, which allegedly disclosed Das' address, drew more than 2.1 million views and was republished August 10, the suit said.

  

Tresspassing the property: Senior Advocate Akhil Sibal, representing Das, said people from The Pamphlet appeared at his property, posted a video that was later reposted by Iyer-Mitra. The suit also alleges Iyer-Mitra disclosed Das' address on a Law Beat program, naming his neighbor and incorrectly stating the rent at about Rs. 7 lakh per month. Iyer-Mitra then published multiple posts repeating the information, according to the suit. Das said he filed a police complaint on August 5.

  

He cited the right to privacy and Press Council of India norms that exclude a public figure's residence, personal finances and parentage from publication. The [Press Council of India's Norm 42](https://presscouncil.nic.in/ViewPdfContent.aspx?Page=DocumentsOfPCI&Title=Norms%20of%20Journalists%20Conduct,%202022) on the right to privacy states that “publishing a person's address in a newspaper when it has no direct relevance to the news report violates their right to privacy.”

  

Senior Advocate Percival Billimoria, appearing for Iyer-Mitra, countered that a Supreme Court ruling in the Puttaswamy case held there is no privacy right over information such as residential address and date of birth. Withoput going into the specific details, Justice Datta appears to have referred to the Supreme Court’s 1994 judgment in [R. Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/501107/) which addressed the right to be let alone. 

  

This is what the Supreme Court had noted as “broad principles” in 1994: 

  

“The right to privacy is implicit in the right to life and liberty guaranteed to the citizens of this country by Article 21. It is a "right to be let alone". A citizen has a right to safeguard the privacy of his own, his family, marriage, procreation, motherhood, child-bearing and education among other matters. None can publish anything concerning the above matters without his consent whether truthful or otherwise and whether laudatory or critical. If he does so, he would be violating the right to privacy of the person concerned and would be liable in an action for damages.” – [Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy (SC, 1994)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/501107/)

Meanwhile, the Puttaswamy judgment addresses residential addresses and personal identifiers like dates of birth under informational privacy, focusing on the state’s ability to collect such data under specific conditions if the balancing test is satisfied. Residential addresses and other personal details, including fingerprints, photographs, and employment information, are generally protected under the righ to privacy as they are directly linked to the Personally Identifiable Information (PII).
