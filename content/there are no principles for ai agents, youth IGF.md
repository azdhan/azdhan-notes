# ‘There are no principles set in place for AI agents’: A panel at Youth IGF 

  

“There are no principles set in place specifically for AI agents,” said Gowree Gokhale, an independent lawyer, to MediaNama after a panel discussion on ‘Cybersecurity in the Age of AI’ at the Youth Internet Governance Forum India (IGF) held at IIT Bombay on September 4, 2026. 

Ghokhale made this remark after the youth-led stakeholder consultation session on cybersecurity and AI. During the second half of the forum, the organizers posed the following hypothetical question to the participants and asked them to come up with stakeholder recommendations:

“If a small nation can be breached in four days by autonomous agents built from free, open parts, with the attacker still uncertain a month later, who is responsible, and what should be done before this happens again?”

Why this question is relevant: Because, recently, the [OpenAI-Hugging Face incident](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-explained-ai-agents-hugging-face-openai/) demonstrated how unpredictable AI-behavior is and how the non-deterministic nature of AI and AI agents is leading us in an uncertain direction. For some context, OpenAI’s AI agents escaped the sandboxed environment to break into Hugging Face and exposed sensitive information like credentials, a repository of AI models. Interestingly, they accomplished it by using the persistent messaging boards to form a collective of bots. While doing this, the AI Agents also broke the OpenAI’s own research infrastructure. 

To discuss this kind of problem, the organizers of Youth IGF posed that hypothetical question and later divided the participants into four stakeholder groups:

- Government and Regulators
    
- Private Sector
    
- Civil Society and Users
    
- Technical Community
    

As MediaNama’s editor Nikhil Pahwa often recalls a remark his friend made from a panel discussion almost 10 years ago: "Government always needs a throat to choke." A young representative from the made-up government stakeholder group explicitly said, “We want a due-diligence regime to assign liability for each person in the chain.” (A non-exhaustive list of suggested recommendations is at the end of the article.)

In this context of liability and the unpredictable AI agentic actions, especially when they go rogue/haywire, in less or non-existent regulatory frameworks, finding the exact liability trace will be a hard law enforcement problem. 

The intermediary liability or safe harbor doctrines might not be sufficient to deal with AI agents. Because, the [“nature and service”](https://www.medianama.com/2026/07/223-meity-ai-platforms-safe-harbor/) of the AI agents are not in alignment with how safe harbor protections are weighed under the  [IT Act’s Section 79 2(b).](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/show-data?actid=AC_CEN_45_76_00001_200021_1517807324077&orderno=105) AI platforms are counted as intermediaries only if they don't:

- “Initiate the transmission,
    
- select the receiver of the transmission, and
    
- select or modify the information contained in the transmission;”
    
- observes due diligence under the IT Act and other guidelines. 
    

  

However, in the case of AI agents, especially the autonomous agents, the initiation and reception of transmission of information may not originate from the user (prompter) all the time. The autonomous AI agent selects and modifies information and takes decisions to act upon by design.  

“There are no principles set in place specifically for AI agents,” said Gowree to MediaNama after the discussion. She further added that if the AI companies are held liable for the actions of their AI agents, they will not get the safe harbor protections from the day one itself. She said that the company has to prove its involvement or non-involvement in the concerned actions with the technical proof if the company has to avoid the liability. 

“In India, we largely lack accountability toward individuals at an individual level. I just wanted to put that perspective on the table,” added Gokhale elsewhere during the discussion, referring to the previous case in Delhi HC where the global settings/policies of a tech company were not being implemented in Indian jurisdictions.

Drawing parallels with the AI agentic behavior and addressing how the young people’s behavior at the stakeholder discussion, Saikat Dutta, Co-Founder & CEO, DeepStrat & RiskStrat, made a very interesting observation: 

The AI agentic behavior resembles human behavior: “All of you [stakeholders] behaved like AI agents. Let me unpack that. When different groups or stakeholders came and spoke, how many of you actually engaged with the scenario you were given? What most people did instead was create their own dynamic and, within that, look at what the other group's dynamic was. So it became this agent-versus-agent exercise. Which is why the facilitator eventually said, ‘This isn't a competition. You need to figure out how to work together.’”

Shreya Krishnan, MD of AnitaB, also made a similar observation but in a different context. She said that human biases and faults eventually creep into AI systems as well, like sexism, and therefore we need to solve the problems at the roots first. Referring to such precautionary measures, one young female participant asked, "Why can’t we stop the AI models from giving the responses to the harmful/dangerous/illegal actions?" Responding to this, Saikat again drew parallels with general human behavior. 

“Why do we even call it ‘artificial intelligence’? Because we're attributing it a degree of sentience; we're saying we've given it, or think we've given it, the ability to make some autonomous decisions on its own. Very simply put: If you try to just tell it not to do something, that's like telling a child, "Don't have ice cream" — but the child will still have the ice cream, because they've had it before, they love the taste, and they know the sugar rush that follows. So the same strategies we use to manage a child are, in many ways, the strategies we'll need with AI.

“It's easy to say ‘guardrails here, guardrails there,' but when theory meets reality, we need much more realistic approaches to actually reach the outcomes we want,” he added.

On nation-gating of frontier AI models: First of all, if I want to use, say, Claude Opus version 6 or version 7, is it even available to me? Companies are being very selective about who gets access. That creates two problems, both serious. First, if I don't have access, I'm already several steps behind those who do—not just because I'm missing out on using it to uncover more vulnerabilities, but because I'm missing out on the knowledge that comes from using it. Every time you deploy and prompt a model like Opus, you get better and better at using it. I can't do that if someone's standing at the gate saying, "You can come in today, but not for the next 10 days."

“Every time you do a Google search, which country produces the most journals, books or PDFs that show up? The U.S., right? So whatever we're afraid AI will do to us has already been happening for the past 20 or 30 years, because one geographic and linguistic entity has been dominating how we think.” – Saikat Dutta. 

The risks of giving our data to foreign AI models: “The second problem is that if I do get access, I'm uploading a lot of data that's specific to our regulatory environment and context. And the company providing that access may, in turn, share that same knowledge with its home country, which could use it against us. So where do you draw the guardrails? As a result, what's happening in India today is that we're not only losing out on knowledge and struggling to innovate; we're also opening ourselves up to risk.” 

“For example, everyone got excited about social media, but social media turned into a giant data-extraction tool for a handful of companies, which then repurposed that data. If you've put your photos on social media, those faces are being extracted for facial-recognition training and facial-reconstruction models, among other things,” he further added. 

“Cybersecurity issues exist with or without AI, and we haven't solved the problem either way” - Gowree Gokhale, Independent Lawyer 

“What AI has done is create a certain mass and a certain speed to these problems.”  - Saikat Dutta, Co-Founder & CEO, DeepStrat & RiskStrat

One problem in the tech industry: “The answer isn't always speed [to fix the security patches],  it's a proper understanding of what you're dealing with before you act. Is it a code problem? A process problem? Is it two APIs talking to each other in a way that's causing the issue? That has to be carefully thought through.” Dutta said this in the context of referring to an instance where one of their clients created a much bigger problem in the process of quickly solving/patching a smaller one. 

A non-exhaustive list of recommendations suggested by the respective youth-led stakeholder groups:

1. Make incident attribution traceable, even for autonomous attacks. Internal access should be logged for both human and agentic actors. (Technical Community, Government)
    
2. Establish a clear liability chain for AI-driven attacks. Hold both the person who launched the agents and the developer who wrote the code accountable. (Government, Private Sector, Civil Society)
    
3. Protect civil liberties in any response. No sweeping powers, no emergency over-correction that stays in place after the crisis ends. (Civil Society)
    
4. Rebuild public trust by being transparent and owning up to the breach instead of denying it. (Civil Society)
    
5. Build technical safeguards into AI systems: guardrails, a human in the loop at every step of an agent's action chain, and pattern detection across action sequences. (Technical Community, Private Sector)
    
6. Set up a multi-stakeholder expert body (industry, civil society, government, technical) to advise on guardrails, failures, and prevention. (Government)
    
7. Require a graded due-diligence regime that assigns liability per actor in the supply chain. (Government)
    
8. Set up a victim compensation framework. (Government)
    
9. Set up an incident-monitoring system that defines where responsibility lies in the supply chain. (Government)
    
10. Require one national standard for breach response across the private sector, with safe harbor for firms that report malicious use cases in good faith. (Private Sector)
    
11. Push for international agreements on mandatory logging, traceability, and information sharing among developers of autonomous AI systems. (Private Sector)
    
12. Do not ban open source. Defenders rely on the same tool. (Private Sector, Civil Society)
    
13. Push for data minimization so future leaks do less harm. (Civil Society)
    
14. Keep data local for any AI development that touches critical infrastructure. (Government)
    
15. Require companies to disclose breaches promptly. (Government)
    
16. Require transparency in the datasets used to train models. (Technical Community)
    
17. Demand transparency from government, the private sector, and technical institutions on the actions being taken. (Civil Society)
    

When Gowree Ghokle asked the participants to raise their hands if they believe they know who can solve the cybersecurity problem in India, participants expressed  limited confidence in legal or judicial solutions to cybersecurity issues; most participants saw tech companies and public awareness as potential solutions.
