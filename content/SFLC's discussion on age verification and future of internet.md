# Privacy-preserving age verification is a contradiction, says EDRi policy advisor at SFLC discussion


Anonymity and tamper-proofing in age verification technology against each other by design and therefore states like European Union offering the so-called privacy preserving solutions wouldn't work explained, Simeon de Brouwer, a Policy Advisor at European Digital Rights (EDRi). 

"If the tool is really anonymity-preserving, if it were that good, it would still allow, and it would even more so allow, the circumvention, because no one knows who is verifying their age, only that someone is verifying their age and that this is legit," Simeon said. "And therefore, a stranger could verify their age on my phone and we would be done with it. So it's either/or," he said during an online discussion on "What Age Assurance means for the future of digital rights, online safety and the internet at large." You can watch the live streamed version of the Zoom discussion [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=euSkCvhzweQ), on YouTube.

The panel had over 15 speakers and several made interesting points advocating for online privacy, some of the interesting points made at the discussion are as follows: 

 Addressing the recent global policy shift in social media bans for children and therefore relying on the age verification and user identification mechanism, Mishi Choudhary, the founder of Software Freedom Law Centre, India (SFLC.in), said that "it sounds like a small design change, but it changes the relationship between users, platforms, and governments." 

During the discussion, Michael A. Specter, Assistant Professor at the Georgia Institute of Technology presented his findings after reverse engineering one of the popular age verification platform. He stated the following points: 
- **Only 14% of the websites have age verification mechanism:** " The vast majority of sites that were actually required by law to have them, that were actually self-marked as being required by law to have some age verification system in place, actually just don't bother at all. So of those [that] self-mark, only 14% actually use age verification."
- **People can simply migrate to platforms without safeguards before even technical bypasses:** "Others here spoke about bypassing the technical measures involved, but we actually just don't have to reach this. People will just go to a different site that just aren't going to comply. And if you think about it, [it] actually isn't that surprising. There's an inherent incentive, if a lot of the bigger actors that are more obvious pull out of the market or require an age verification gate, to accept the traffic that would be given up by them."
- **There is a concentration of age verification platforms:** "There's a lot of concentration in the market. Over 60% of the sites we examined use one provider called Yoti. That same provider is also used by several large social media companies. I believe Meta uses them. I believe TikTok uses them. And various others." He further argued that these platforms with such control and power could control what users can access and possible violate their privacy rights too. 

"Our [Australia] government has, to mix my metaphors, used the bluntest of instruments, prohibition, to hit the lowest of hanging fruit, being social media access, as opposed to properly defining what the problem is in the first place, and realising that there are risks and harms created by social media that don't apply exclusively to children and young people but to all people, and that the solution is not prohibition," said John Pane, Chair at Electronic Frontiers Australia. 

**On what should be the approach if social media is being compared with smoking or drinking:** Taking from the popular parlance of comparing social media with smoking and drinking, Pane said that unlike US, UK and Australia, which have stricter alcohol prohibition, European countries introduce alcohol at the family meals therefore amking them more aware of the harms and thus encouraging responsibility. 

"So what the government is trying to fix by banning is putting young children in a place where it is removing their agency, removing their ability to flourish in a digital environment, an environment which most of us adults probably weren't brought up with, certainly our older adults, and also creating harms and unintended consequences for certain subpopulations," he further stated. 

**How user identification for social media posts backfired in South Korea:** K.S. Park from Open Net Korea shared the anecdotal experience of how mandating linking of ID similar to Social Scurity Number had backfired in South Korea while increasing the illegal online content, which led to courts struck down of te law in 2012 in the favour of data minimisation principles as the linking of each social media profile to post anything on internet has rapidly increased the data collection, 

"The proportion of illegal content actually increased after the institution of this law, because due to concerns about privacy, and due to the lack of, or due to the [reliance] on, the right to anonymous communication, a lot of people stopped writing online. And the bad actors who are doing intentional illegal acts online continued to do the same thing using somebody else's Social Security numbers. So as the numerator stayed constant, the denominator shrank. In some cases, independent media outlets suffered a decrease in their postings by [as much as] one-twentieth of what it used to be. So in terms of the proportion of illegal content, it rather increased. So the law didn't accomplish the goal, the stated goal," he recalled. 

**Social media restrictions are not age assuring measures, they are digital ID mechanisms**: "I think things like age assurance are industry marketing terms. Assurance sounds nice; we're assured of it. I think we should be calling it what it is, which is a form of digital ID, and we should be calling these age gates a digital ID gate that stops you from accessing things freely. It's a block on your [free expression]. You have to hand over some of your personal details in some form to do that. And as soon as your personal details get associated with other bits of information about you, that is forming an online ID profile," stated James Baker of  UK-based privacy and free speech organisation, Open Rights Group.

David Sullivan from Digital Trust and Safety Partnership
 noted that the work is already shaping formal international benchmarks, pointing to [ISO 27566-1](https://www.iso.org/standard/88143.html), a framework for age assurance that was published in late 2025. For Sullivan, this signals that a mature, consensus-based alternative to heavy-handed mandates already exists.
