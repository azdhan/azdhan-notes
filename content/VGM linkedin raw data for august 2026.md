# top 2 

Stop asking PR people for pay-as-you-go PR.  
  
PR isn't a vending machine.  
  
Everyone who reaches out saying we will pay you if you get us one article in NYT or Forbes, what you're really asking for is: give me the outcome without giving me the massive effort.  
  
The hard truth is 👉 good PR takes months to compound. The first 3-4 weeks are spent understanding your story, building narrative, updating the media list, and active pitching.  
  
To land one article in a publication like The New York Times, means weeks of work to:  
  
→ find the exact journalist(s) who will cover the story  
→ tailor the story angle sharply to this journalist  
→ write, re-write, and multiple pitch approvals  
→ follow-ups + pitching other outlets  
→ pivot the pitch for another journo  
  
Good PR agencies will structure pitching such that you don't only land the NYT coverage but multiple others along with it.  
  
✅ Showing trust in your PR agency or consultant is half the battle won as it boosts their confidence and creativity, which leads to winning pitches.  
  
❌ Paying only

for the outcome shows lack of trust and builds unnecessary pressure, which stifles creativity.  
  
If you want quick hits, that's advertising. If you want credibility that actually moves the needle for your business, that's PR.  
  
And when has credibility ever been built on quick fixes?  
  
PR folks, do you agree?

# top 3 


Unpopular PR opinion: 

I only write PR pitches for the biggest outlets when I'm in a really good mood.

Say what you will, but I know that energy passes on to my pitches — I feel confident that it will land. 

Does that mean I don't pitch if I don't feel great? Absolutely not. 

👉 Some days, it can mean writing a few other pitches before I reach that level of confidence for a larger outlet. 

👉 Other days, it might mean pitching right after my morning coffee when my mind is the sharpest. 

Pitching when I am completely present reflects in my clarity of thought, story angle editing, and writing. 

As a result, I guarantee that journalists can see how that pitch stands out from others (I was one so I know good writing when I see it). 

If the pitch lands, which it does 7/10 times, I know it worked. 

If it doesn't, I still know I did my best work. 

Pitching is a creative exercise and turning it into an algorithm will make your pitch like the 200 other pitches the journalist deleted that day. 

So, my mantra ➡️ pitch with a great mood than lose the story altogether.

# top 4 

You're wasting PR if you stop at getting coverage.  
  
PR only pays off when you do the following 5 things AT LEAST:  
  
1️⃣ Post it on LinkedIn yourself  
Don't wait for people to stumble on it. Write a real take on why the story matters, not just "excited to share this feature."  
  
2️⃣ Add it to your pitch decks  
A credible third party validating you is worth being seen by investors/clients.  
  
3️⃣ Bring it up in sales calls  
"We were just covered in XYZ for this exact problem" creates curiosity.  
  
4️⃣ Add it to your email signature  
Low effort, high visibility. Every email becomes a small trust signal.  
  
5️⃣ Put it on your website's press page  
If a prospect is doing diligence on you, this is where they'll look. An empty or outdated press page quietly costs you credibility.  
  
The story is the raw material. What you build with it after is the actual PR.  
  
If you're only screenshotting your coverage and forgetting it, you're leaving a ton of value on the table.

Before signing on a PR agency, each client needs to ask ONE question:

"How involved am I willing to be?"

One of the biggest misconceptions about PR is that you hire an agency, disappear, and then wake up to media coverage.

It doesn't work like that.

The best PR results I've ever delivered has always been a collaboration.

Here's what involved clients do differently:

→ They respond quickly to media requests.
→ They make time for interviews.
→ They will even give you names of publications they want to be featured in. 
→ They share opinions instead of asking, "What should I say?"
→ They tell us what's happening inside the business before it becomes public.
→ They trust the process.

PR isn't just about pitching journalists.

It's also about:

→ Being interested in stories. 
→ Responding to deadlines. 
→ Brainstorming story angles with your agency. 
→ Knowing clearly what you want to be known for. 

My involved clients with zero PR have landed features in Business Insider. 

On the other hand, disinterested clients mean a failed relationship. 

To maximize results for my clients, now I always ask: 

"How involved are you willing to be in PR?"

This one question can make all the difference.

# top 6 

Here's THE simplest tip to extract better story angles for your PR clients.

I bet most of you aren't doing this simple thing.

Follow your client's content. Everywhere.

Instagram. Their newsletter. LinkedIn. All of it.

Here's why:

Most PR pros only look at what a client says in briefings and calls. That's the filtered version.

But the real angles are hiding in the everyday content they don't think twice about:

→ The newsletter about a problem they're solving differently 
→ The IG story about a decision they made last-minute 
→ The LinkedIn post that got emotional replies 
→ The story about how they quit their job

Most clients don't think their true story is PR worthy. Your job is to extract that story.

Because journalists don't want the polished pitch. They want the real thing.

Here's an example 👇 

A client of mine developed an app to reward people who shop locally. 

Brilliant idea. 

But you know which story got picked up by a dozen outlets?

She was a 60-year-old grandma who chose to launch a tech business instead of retiring. 

The lesson: your client's product is rarely the angle.

Their story is.

Follow their content and ask the tough questions to find that story.
