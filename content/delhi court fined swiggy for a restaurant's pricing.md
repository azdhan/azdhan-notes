**# Why Delhi Consumer Commission has ruled that Swiggy can’t claim safe harbor protections for Dineout pricing

Access a copy of the order here: [[PDF](https://www.medianama.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/document-6-1-704028.pdf)]

We missed this earlier: Swiggy "cannot escape from its responsibility merely by quoting section 79 of the Information Technology Act," a Delhi consumer commission had said on September 21, 2026, while finding the platform and a restaurant, Themis Barbeque House, jointly responsible for a misleading "cost for two" figure shown on Swiggy Dineout.

“Swiggy cannot escape from its responsibility merely by quoting section 79 of the Information Technology Act. It has to show conclusively that the Swiggy was performing due diligence in all respects as necessitated in the respective *legislations* and rules framed thereunder.” – Delhi’s  consumer commission.

Some background: When the complainant booked a table for 10 people in April 2024 through the Swiggy’s Dineout section, the listing apparently  showed a cost of Rs 1,500 for two, indicating that it would cost Rs. 750 per person. However, the final bill charged Rs. 1,050 for each vegetarian buffet and Rs 1,100 for each non-vegetarian buffet, which is Rs 300 and Rs 350 more per person. Additionally, the petitioner also stated that the bill carried a 10% service charge on beverages, which the restaurant refused to remove. He said his in-app chat complaint to Swiggy got no proper response.

The “Cost for two” figure is a common estimate shown on popular food delivery platforms and   Google Maps as well for select restaurants. 

What did Swiggy argue: It argued that Dineout is only an intermediary facilitating transactions between customers and restaurants, and is not responsible for details communicated by the restaurants. It said the "Rs 1,500 for two" figure was an average or estimate meant to indicate affordability, not exact pricing, and that the detailed menu with the weekend dinner buffet prices was available once a user clicked on the restaurant.

Swiggy also claimed protection under Section 79 of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which exempts an intermediary from liability for third-party information it hosts or transmits, provided it did not initiate the transmission, select the receiver or modify the information, and observed due diligence. It asked for the complaint against it to be dismissed. Meanwhile, the restaurant did not appear or file a written statement and was proceeded against ex parte on February 7, 2025.

What did the court say: The commission stated that the nothing on the Dineout listing indicated that the "cost for two" figure was an estimate, and that [Swiggy's own Terms & Conditions](https://www.swiggy.com/terms-and-conditions) did not describe it as approximate, indicative or non-binding. It held that a price shown before booking, which induces a consumer to book through the platform, creates a reasonable expectation that the final bill will not differ materially.

The commission said the figure was displayed "mischievously" to mislead the complainant about the buffet's true price. It held the restaurant responsible as the owner of the advertisement, and Swiggy as the platform where it was displayed "without being scrutinised."

“The “cost for two” figure displayed on the Swiggy Dineout listing falls within this definition as such listing is clearly displayed mischievously to mislead the Complainant as to the true price of the buffet. Swiggy’s own admission indicates that the menu was available with it at the time when the Complainant made the booking and it shows that accurate, itemised pricing for the weekend buffet was available to it at the material time. Yet the lower figure continued to be displayed without correction or any prominent disclaimer that the prices are different on weekends. For such an incorrect and misleading display, responsibility falls on both [Swiggy and Themis Barbeque House] being the owner of the advertisement and Swiggy being the platform where such misleading advertisement was displayed without being scrutinised.” – Delhi Consumer Court

MediaNama's Take: As it is known and accepted that the intermediaries are granted safe harbor protections when the platform doesn't have much control over the content hosted on their platforms, holding Swiggy liable for the overcharging of a restaurant is questionable. However, the non-disclosures of approximate nature of the "cost for two" is a legitimate legal issue. In fact, the platforms should display the approximate nature of such labels quite explicitly instead of hiding them under the terms and conditions. (Swiggy hasn't specified this even in the Terms.)

Moreover, considering the "cost for two" label as the "misleading advertisement" is also not accurate because, even if the Restaurant might be a paying/commission-based client of Swiggy Dineout, the restaurant may not have paid specifically for the "cost for two" label/information. So, why should it be considered as advertisement/commercial speech instead of treating it as a part of free speech/information? 

Why it matters: Extending beyond the food-based platforms, the intermediary liability of the e-commerce platforms and the social media platforms has been questioned and the judiciary/consumer protection bodies have held the platforms accountable for the content/products on their platforms. In June 2026,  Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) sent Blinkit two notices within two weeks over consumer complaints about spoiled food. Quick-commerce platforms generally can't claim the intermediary liability if they are running the warehouse. However, Blinkit's own terms of service still position it as a neutral marketplace claiming that it is not responsible for the "any transaction between you and the Third Party Sellers.”

Similarly, other e-commerce platforms/marketplaces have also been for not maintaining certain due diligence. A few of such instances are: 

- Flipkart fined for the non-BIS toys and 'Flipkart Assured' tags: In August 2026, Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) has [imposed](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-ccpa-flipkart-penalty-non-bis-toys/) a Rs 5 lakh penalty on Flipkart for facilitating the sale of toys on its platform that did not comply with mandatory Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) requirements.
    

- Amazon and Flipkart fined for the sale of walkie-talkies: In January 2026, CCPA [fined](https://www.medianama.com/2026/01/223-ccpa-flipkart-meta-illegal-walkie-talkies-sale/) both Flipkart and Amazon for the sale of walkie-talkies that doesn't have required regulatory disclosures. 
    
- Meta Marketplace argues CCPA has no jurisdiction over the intermediary: that Similarly, when Meta was [fined](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-meta-ccpa-no-jurisdiction-facebook-marketplace-listings-walkie-talkie-fine/) for the sale of walkie-talkies on its marketplace, it challenged the order arguing that CCPA had no jurisdiction over Facebook Marketplace and consumer protection [guidelines](https://www.consumerprotection.in/consumer-protection-e-commerce-rules-2020/) are not applicable to it since the platform is not a traditional e-commerce platform and does not directly sell products.
    

  

The Commission based the jusgement on the following Acts, Rules and Guidelines: 

Section 79 of the Information Technology Act, 2000: The commission called Swiggy's reliance on Section 79 "misconceived." It said the exemptions are conditional on the requirements, which requires an intermediary to "observe due diligence while discharging his duties." 

“Swiggy has placed nothing on record showing it maintains any mechanism to reconcile the prices listed under the “price for two” figure against actual billing.”

As specified above, it stated that an intermediary should be able to show "conclusively" that it hadt performed due diligence in all respects required under the relevant laws and rules.

Consumer Protection Act, 2019: The commission said the listing fell within the definition of a "misleading advertisement" in Section 2(28). It applied Section 2(47), noting two limbs of the "unfair trade practice" definition as relevant: a statement that materially misleads the public about the price at which services are ordinarily provided (clause 1(i)), and permitting an advertisement at a price that is not in fact intended to be honoured at the point of sale (clause 2).

“‘cost for two’ figure on the Swiggy Dineout platform, being materially at variance with the amount actually billed, and its continued display of that figure despite having access to accurate, itemised pricing, amounts to deficiency in service and unfair trade practice within the meaning of Sections 2(11) and 2(47) of the Act for which both the OPs are equally responsible.” 

Consumer Protection (E-commerce) Rules, 2020: The commission said the Rules make e-commerce platforms responsible for ensuring that information displayed by sellers is accurate and corresponds to the goods or services actually offered. 

“The price difference that the Complainant has suffered because of inaccurate display of the price on the platform owned and operated by Swiggy is a classic example of failure on part of Swiggy in ensuring correctness of the advertisement.” 

Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022: The commission said the Guidelines place responsibility for preventing misleading advertisements equally on the service provider and the advertiser. It further said that the Guidelines and the Act: 

“...fixes the responsibility for prevention of misleading advertisement equally to the product manufacturer/ service provider advertising their product/ service and the advertiser. In the case in hand, Themis Barbeque House is the service provider which put its advertisement on Swiggy’s platform and Themis Barbeque House is the advertiser. The said guidelines fixes responsibility on both and none of these parties can skip from their respective responsibilities.” 

Are intermediaries not liable for the ads on their platform? That is debatable. In a separate case, when Internet Freedom Foundation's (IFF) Apar Gupta was [arguing](https://thewire.in/government/ignorance-is-not-an-excuse-in-law) in 2016 that 'search engines are considered intermediaries while displaying search results that result from automated indexing but that they aren’t intermediaries when they accept publish paid for advertisements,' an Intellectual property lawyer, Prashant Reddy, wrote on [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2016/08/do-online-advertising-platforms-qualify-for-intermediary-liability-protection.html) arguing: 

"While the search engines operated by Google or Yahoo most certainly fall within the definition of intermediaries while they operate in their capacity as search engines, they certainly do not qualify as intermediaries when they publish advertisements. When Google AdWords publishes advertisements, it is no longer an intermediary – it falls squarely within the definition of a publisher. Like any other publisher, it actively solicits advertisements from clients, negotiates rates for each advertisement, it follows an active editorial policy which you can find over [here](https://support.google.com/adwordspolicy/answer/6008942?hl=en) and pre-screens advertisements as per this policy before it makes the decision to publish the advertisement online – most of the pre-screening is automatic, while some of it does manually – this mode of functioning ensures that Google has knowledge of everything that it publishes. Google Ad-words is therefore no longer a platform facilitating automatic transactions – it is a publisher and should be held liable like any other publisher who has prior knowledge of the content that it is publishing." – Lawyer Prashant Reddy, [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2016/08/do-online-advertising-platforms-qualify-for-intermediary-liability-protection.html).

The liability of platforms for the alleged illegal/CSAM ads run on their platforms is also relevant in the case of [Meta hosting CSAM-related ads on their platform(s),](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-meta-ai-generated-csam-ads-india-investigation/) like Instagram and Facebook. In August 2026, the Centre has [reportedly](https://www.livemint.com/technology/centre-tells-meta-safe-harbour-wont-cover-csam-violations-calls-for-increased-sensitivity-towards-indias-cultural-linguistic-context-govt-sources-11787035907575.html) told that 'safe harbor protections will not be available to individuals or persons involved' in CSAM violations. 

Finding both of the parties liable, the Commision finally ruled: 

  

- Swiggy and the restaurant both have to jointly pay Rs 1 lakh for the mental agony, harassment and inconvenience caused, plus litigation costs. 
    
- Themis Barbeque House has to pay Rs. 3,988 for ’overcharging’ along with the Rs. 254 falsely levied as service charge on the beverage bill, both carrying 9% annual interest from April 7, 2024.
    
- They have four weeks to pay up and if they miss that deadline they have to pay 12% annual interest over the total fine amount.**
