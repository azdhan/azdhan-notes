### What is NATGRID?

NATGRID stands for National Intelligence Grid is a public-private counter-terrorism initiative from the central governement which aims to combine both public and private datasets and make them available to the intelligence agencies for strengthening of "national security."

The government obtains the power to surveil and intercept communications, both online and telecom-based, through the archaic Section 5(2) of the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885.


The Act specifically states three things:

- In the name of "public emergency" or "public safety" is at risk, the central or state government may temporarily take control of any licensed telegraph system if it believes the action is necessary.
    
- The government may also order certain messages to be blocked, intercepted, delayed, or shared with officials if necessary to protect India’s sovereignty, security, foreign relations, or public order, or to prevent criminal incitement. Such orders must be recorded in writing.
    
- However, messages from accredited press correspondents meant for publication in India cannot be stopped or intercepted unless their transmission has been specifically banned.

### Why is NATGRID is Privacy Concern?

In 1999, the People’s Union of Civil Liberties (PUCL) [filed](https://privacylibrary.ccgnlud.org/case/pucl-vs-union-of-india) a petition in the Supreme Court of India, challenging Section 5(2) of the Telegraph Act, arguing that intercepting communication systems constitutes a violation of individual privacy.

  

The Court held that the privacy of telephone conversations forms part of the right to privacy under Article 21, and that phone-tapping is unconstitutional unless authorised by a valid “procedure established by law.” It further noted that telephone conversations are an exercise of free speech under Article 19(1)(a); therefore, any interception must qualify as a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2).

  

"Telephone-Tapping is a serious invasion of an individual's privacy. With the growth of highly sophisticated communication technology, the right to hold telephone conversation, in the privacy of one's home or office without interference, is increasingly susceptible to abuse. It is no doubt correct that every Government, howsoever democratic, exercises some degree of subrosa operation as a part of its intelligence out-fit but at the same time citizen's right to privacy has to be protected from being abused by the authorities of the day." - [Supreme Court of India.](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/31276692/)

  

While the court didn't strike down Section 5(2), it noted that the provision clearly laid down conditions under which interception orders could be given, with the first step being to satisfy two prerequisites:

- ‘occurrence of any public emergency’ or
- ‘the interest of public safety’,

The authorised Government officer then has to be satisfied that it was “necessary or expedient” in the interest of five grounds enumerated under this section, which are:

- Sovereignty and integrity of India;
- Security of the State;
- Friendly relations with foreign States;
- Public order; or
- Preventing incitement to the commission of an offence.

Additionally, the court ordered the authorities to maintain a detailed record of intercepted communications. 

### The Flawed History of Indian Government Surveillance

Having an advanced national intelligence system to strengthen the national security measures should always be welcomed. However, the problem lies in the vagueness of how national security can be interpreted and the broadness of its consequences.

For example, in the Bhima Koregaon case, which incarcerated over 16 individuals merely for conducting a rally [](https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/op-ed/understanding-bhima-koregaon/article22361017.ece)in favour of the 1818 Battle of Bhima Koregaon, where Mahar soldiers (Dalits) of the British East India Company defeated the Peshwa's army, symbolising a victory over caste oppression by the high-caste Peshwas. 

In this case, according to the interviews conducted by, London School of Economics anthropology professor Alpa Shah for her book titled The Incarcerations: Bhima Koregaon And The Search For Democracy In India, she [claims](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/hacker-for-hire-gang-with-links-to-pune-police-planted-emails-on-the-computers-of-bhima-koregaon-accused-new-book/article67946916.ece) that the Pune police officer allegedly hired hackers to plant digital "evidence" in at least three laptops of the 16 accused to implicate them in the case.

In addition to the recent and ongoing Pegasus controversy, where the Supreme Court found evidence of spyware on at least 5 of the 29 phones examined in a case against the Indian Government for allegedly purchasing zero-click spyware from Israel’s NSO Group, used to snoop on journalists, opposition politicians, and academicians, there was also a [2009 investigation by CobraPost and the now-defunct Gulail.com](https://www.business-standard.com/article/politics/all-you-need-to-know-about-snoopgate-113112700241_1.html) revealing that Amit Shah, then BJP National General Secretary and now Union Home Minister, had ordered illegal surveillance on a Gujarat-based landscape architect referred to as “Madhuri,” a pseudonym used by journalists to protect her identity
  
Based on 267 audio recordings that were handed over to the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI), which investigative journalists had access to, it was revealed that Amit Shah ordered the illegal surveillance against her based on orders from "Saheb." While there is no conclusive evidence to prove that the saheb in reference was then Gujarat CM Narendra Modi, may critics believed so. Nonetheless, after nearly six years, in 2021, the Supreme Court [gave](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/snoopgate-delete-allegations-against-modi-says-sc/article5586563.ece) PM Modi a clean chit and asked the CBI to remove “scurrilous” allegations against him.

The most important aspect of this incident is that no one denied the surveillance against the pseudonymous woman. More bizarrely, Rajnath Singh claimed that her father himself requested surveillance against his daughter. 

"The girl's father has written a letter in this context (snooping row). It is all clear. I feel that a father is always more concerned for his daughter. Therefore, I feel that no question should be raised over the issue," he reportedly said, as [documented](https://www.business-standard.com/article/politics/all-you-need-to-know-about-snoopgate-113112700241_1.html) [[archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20251208192851/https://www.business-standard.com/article/politics/all-you-need-to-know-about-snoopgate-113112700241_1.html)] by Business Standard. 

It is vital to note that even parents don't have a legal right to surveil their kids if the children are capable and independent adults.
