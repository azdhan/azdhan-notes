# Stanford undergraduates deserve nudges they can see and trust

**Residential and Dining Enterprises already decides what is easy to eat. It should make that choice healthy, fair and open.**

Eating at Stanford is mandatory in a way classes are not. Residential and Dining Enterprises, known as R&DE, requires almost all undergraduates living on campus to buy a dining hall plan. Students choose 19, 15 or 12 meals per week in the halls, which is simply how many swipes they get. All three cost the same $8,142 total for 2026-27 on [R&DE's official meal plan page](https://rde.stanford.edu/dining-hospitality/dining-hall-meal-plans), with the 15 meal plan as the default. Swipes cover the halls. A smaller pot called Dining Dollars covers retail counters.

Because students cannot opt out, placement and price matter more than advice. When Arrillaga and Lakeside shorten hours, dinner moves to Tresidder Memorial Union. Last fall students [told The Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2024/10/30/stanford-hungry-for-healthier-options/) that shift left them with fast food defaults and a $16 burrito as the fresher buy. That is the whole case in one night. What is open and what Dollars cover decides what students eat.

For a university that helped write the guide to this problem, the fix is within reach.

R&DE Stanford Dining, Hospitality and Auxiliaries co-founded the [Menus of Change University Research Collaborative](https://www.moccollaborative.org/) and published the [Food Choice Architecture Playbook](https://rde.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/2025-01/FoodChoice%20ArchitecturePlaybook2025.pdf) on making healthy prominent. That skill shows in special diets. [Branner Dining Hall](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/01/branner-allergen-free-dining) cooks free of the nine major allergens. [Florence Moore](https://rde.stanford.edu/dining-hospitality/glatt-kosher-dining-program) hosts kosher service with halal options on request. The gap is the regular line.

That reframes the three familiar demands into one job: design the default well.

First, let a dietitian set the default. [Eat Well at Stanford](https://rde.stanford.edu/dining-hospitality/eat-well-stanford) lists [Kristen Gravani, MS, RDN](https://rde.stanford.edu/dining-hospitality/eat-well-stanford) and a nutrition team that reviews purchases and labeling. That review helps students who request allergy plans. Most students eat the standard rotation and Late Night. R&DE already places vegetables first, puts the salad bar at the door [to guide healthier picks](https://stanforddaily.com/2026/06/01/contract-negotiations-new-policies/), and blends burgers with 30 percent mushrooms. It should publish the dietitian targets behind those moves for regular meals, in plain sodium, fiber and fat language with the month checked, and keep the [Teaching Kitchen](https://rde.stanford.edu/dining-hospitality/teaching-kitchen) focused on keeping that rotation varied. A menu built with a dietitian should taste built, not repeated.

Second, keep the price signal on the healthy side. Inside the halls the rule is right. One swipe buys salad or pizza for the same cost. After hours it flips. At [Union Square](https://stanforddaily.com/2026/01/27/all-you-can-eat-does-union-square-work-for-you/) only three counters take Dining Dollars, early closures leave fast food as the late option, and the fresh buy costs extra cash once Dollars run out. Keep one dietitian aligned bowl, meaning grains plus protein plus vegetables, at Tresidder and Late Night for the same Dollar price as the standard combo and keep it open late. Let Dollars work at more counters. Then the easy choice and the affordable choice point the same way.

Third, say what the nudge is. Labels now say what is in the pan, not why it sits first. That silence breaks trust. The [2023 small plates essay](https://stanforddaily.com/2023/10/09/from-the-community-the-big-problem-with-rdes-small-plates/) found the 8 inch plates hold about 60 percent of a standard plate and linked that to hunger and harder days for students with eating disorders. Last November's [plant forward column](https://stanforddaily.com/2025/11/10/all-you-can-eat-stanford-dining-pushes-for-plant-based/) quoted a student saying it should not be easy to accidentally take plant based food. The objection is not to beans. It is to feeling steered without being told. Add one line on the glass for why the burger is blended or the grain is whole, that seconds are normal, and where to reply. Chef Mychel Brewster [holds Tuesday office hours](https://stanforddaily.com/2025/10/29/all-you-can-eat-spend-a-day-with-chef-mychel-brewster/) with a text line and a student committee for feedback. Many students [told The Daily they never knew the feedback portal existed](https://stanforddaily.com/2024/10/30/stanford-hungry-for-healthier-options/). Put it where the line forms.

Picture a sophomore back from practice at 9:30 p.m. Lakeside dark, half the Union Square counters shut, fried food still glowing. She needs the bowl still open to be the one her Dollars cover, with a sign that says why it was built that way.

Stanford has the dietitian, the Food Institute and the Playbook it wrote for others. All the pieces are present. Connect them to dinner as students live it, with standards posted, late prices held fair, and each nudge labeled where it happens.
