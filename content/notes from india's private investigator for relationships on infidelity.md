# The Patterns Nobody Talks About: Notes on Tanya Puri's Infidelity Investigation

**Geographic concentration:** Extramarital cheating is heavily concentrated in metros — Bangalore, Mumbai, Delhi especially. The common factor isn't the city itself but the workspace. If your workplace puts you in constant contact with new people, the possibility multiplies. Open outdoor environments, constant meetings with different people — that's where affairs happen.

**Pattern as destiny:** Second chances don't work because cheating isn't an accident; it's a pattern. And patterns can be monitored. The insight Puri pushes is this: when behavior shifts inconsistently, that's the signal. But here's the thing — you can't just watch the individual's behavior in isolation. You need the family's patterns too. How they move, how they communicate, what's normal for _them_. That's where you catch the lie.

**The technology paradox:** Dating apps and big-city lifestyle make cheating easier to commit and easier to hide. But outdoor, real-life surveillance — the unglamorous detective work — apparently reveals more than online trails ever could. Celebrities cheat more, Puri suggests, because their lifestyle creates both opportunity and plausible deniability. The apps just democratized what celebrities already had.

**Love marriages hold more secrets than arranged ones.** That's a contrarian claim worth sitting with, even if I'm not sure I buy it entirely.

**The emotional-first framework:** Puri distinguishes between emotional and physical infidelity, and argues emotional comes first — a gap, a lack of something the person isn't getting at home. That's when they look elsewhere. Physical cheating follows the emotional one.

**On lies in relationships:** People don't lie most about money. They lie most about past relationships, old connections, lifetime choices — the things that reveal who they were before. The suspicious detail: if it's too good to be true, it probably is.

**Gender observations (with caveats):** Women catch lies more than men do. Women also forgive more and give second chances more often — though Puri frames this as a generalisation and I'm inclined to agree it's messy territory.

**The class myth:** The claim that only rich people cheat doesn't hold. Working-class infidelity exists; it's just hidden differently. **Cheating is a mindset**, not a wealth thing. Some people hide it better because they have fewer eyes on them.

**On the interviewer:** Here's where I want to be direct. The channel — **Vendrama Club**, I think — conducted this with a notable bias. They covered instances of women cheating but immediately moved into nuance and context. When men cheated, it stayed the centrepiece. The interviewer never made the symmetrical point: _men and women both cheat._ It collapsed into a framing where male infidelity was the problem. Also, the questions were often genuinely dumb, and the interviewer's responses missed opportunities to push further. The whole thing needed more rigour.

**Open Questions**

1. What exactly is the mechanism by which family patterns reveal infidelity that individual behavior monitoring doesn't? Is it about baseline-setting, or something else?
2. Why would love marriages generate more secrecy than arranged ones? What's the structural difference?
3. If emotional infidelity comes first, how do you measure or prove it from outside?
4. How much of the gender difference in lie-catching and forgiveness is real pattern versus cultural script?
5. What's the actual difference between how working-class and wealthy people hide affairs — is it just fewer surveillance opportunities, or something deeper?
