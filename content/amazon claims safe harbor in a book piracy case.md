 **# Amazon claims safe-harbour protection at Karnataka HC over a book piracy case

  

Amazon argued before the Karnataka High Court that it qualifies for safe-harbour protection under Section 79 of the Information Technology Act, 2000, in a case involving the alleged sale of pirated copies of the late journalist Ravi Belagere’s novel on its platform. [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/high-court/karnataka-high-court/karnataka-high-court-heli-hogu-kaarana-amazon-547616) reported this development first. 

## Some Background: Bhavana Belagere, daughter of the late journalist Ravi Belagere, who claims copyright over the book “Heli Hogu Kaarana”, filed an FIR filed at a Subramanyapura Police Station. These court proceesinfs are part of this case.

Amazon followed due diligence and thus immunity from liability: Meesho and Flipkart are also named in the FIR but are not part of this proceedings. Advocate Sandesh J. Chouta, representing Amazon, stated that the company operates as a marketplace connecting sellers and buyers and cannot be held liable for copyright infringement by third-party sellers on its platform. He emphasized that Amazon had fulfilled due-diligence obligations regarding sellers on its platform.

Amazon proactively removed illegal sellers after the ‘knowledge’: He cited Sections 79(2) and 79(3) of the IT Act, arguing that Amazon qualifies as an intermediary and is therefore protected from civil or criminal liability under the safe-harbour provision. Chouta also submitted that Amazon had proactively removed the listings of all 12 sellers involved in the alleged infringement as soon as it was notified. He argued that intermediaries are not required to take down listings unless ordered under Section 79(3)(b) of the IT Act.

Amazon distinguished between inventory-based e-commerce models, where the company purchases, stores, and prices products, and marketplace models, where the platform connects sellers and buyers. Chouta stated:

"Blinkit and others follow the inventory model. Flipkart, Amazon, we are just providing a platform." – Advocate Sandesh J. Chouta (Amazon representative) 

Amazon further argued that it could not be named as an accused in the absence of specific allegations that it had failed to comply with due-diligence obligations. The company also provided the Investigating Officer with details of the sellers allegedly involved in the sale of the disputed books.

He also cited the Delhi High Court’s 2020 decision in Amazon v. Amway ([order pdf](https://www.livelaw.in/pdf_upload/pdf_upload-369743.pdf)) where the court rejected the distinction between "active" and "passive" intermediaries for the purpose of safe-harbour protection. This judgement came after Delhi HC’s earlier judgement in 2018’ Christian Louboutin vs Darveys.com case.(More about these cases below.)

When Flipkart was fined for non-BIS toys in its platform: Recently, On Augusat 19, Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) had [fined](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-ccpa-flipkart-penalty-non-bis-toys/) Flipkart Rs. 5 lakh for facilitating the sale of toys that did not comply with Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) requirements under the Toys (Quality Control) Order, 2020, which became mandatory from January 1, 2021. You can access the court order here: [[pdf](https://www.medianama.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/1787472121_flipkart-ccpa.pdf)]

As part of the jusgement, the CCPA had rejected Flipkart’s claim to Section 79 safe harbour, citing the following provisions: 

- Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and 
    
- Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, 
    

Platform assurances may breach the intermediary ‘passive-ness’: However, in the same judgement, it argued that these impose platform-level obligations and are independent of the IT Act’s safe-harbour framework. The order notices that tags like "Flipkart Assured," "Best Seller," "Trending," and "AD," stating that the "Flipkart Assured" tag could imply that Flipkart had verified the product’s safety and quality, undermining its claim to neutral hosting.

However, it is important to note that the CCPA had fined under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and its E-Commerce Rules and did not rule that section 79 does not apply to e-commerce marketplaces generally or E-Commerce Rules displace/overpower the Section 79.

Similarly, in another case, on August 4, 2026, the CCPA [pointed out](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-ccpa-amazon-ram-mandir-prasad-fine/) (o[rder PDF](https://www.medianama.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Amazon_Ram_Mandir_order.pdf)) misleading listings for sweets marketed as "Shri Ram Mandir Ayodhya Prasad" on Amazon. The CCPA held that the E-Commerce Rules impose platform-level consumer-protection duties that operate alongside the Section 79 safe-harbour framework. 

Why it matters: The particular order creates tension with the Supreme Court’s framework in Shreya Singhal judgement, which interpreted Section 79(3)(b) to mean that an intermediary’s "actual knowledge" which the platform can only get through a court order or a notification by the appropriate government or its agency. The Flipkart order treats actual knowledge of non-compliant physical goods as sufficient to invoke liability on the intermediaries if the concerned due diligence as per the rules are not followed.

Under the [IT Act](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/13116/1/it_act_2000_updated.pdf), to claim safe harbor protections, an intermediary should not:

1. Initiate the transmission.
    
2. Select the receiver of the transmission.
    
3. Select or modify the information in the transmission.
    
4. Fail to observe due diligence under the IT Act and other guidelines.
    

Additionally, while arguing that intermediary liability is argued on a case-by-case, legal scholar Vasudev Devadasan, stated that E-Commerce Rules acknowledge the online marketplaces like Amazon and Flipkart are eligible for safe harbor protections under the IT Act. Read his free-to-access paper on [“Intermediary Liability in India” here.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4343781)

  

When does a passive intermediary become an active intermediary and thus inviting liability (later overturned by Amazon and Amway judgement): In November , 2018, while hearing the [Christian Louboutin vs Darveys.com](https://www.medianama.com/2018/11/223-ecommerce-safe-harbor-intermediary-liability-darveys-christian-louboutin/) case, the Delhi High Court examined when an e-commerce platform loses safe harbour under Section 79. 

  

In its order. The HC gave a non-exhaustive list of factors indicating active participation of a platform. They are:

1. Identifying the seller and providing seller details.
    
2. Providing transport for the seller to send products to the platform’s warehouse.
    
3. Uploading product entries.
    
4. Providing quality assurance after reviewing the product.
    
5. Providing authenticity guarantees.
    
6. Creating product listings.
    
7. Providing or uploading product reviews.
    
8. Enrolling members upon payment of membership fees.
    
9. Promoting products among its customer database.
    
10. Advertising products on the platform.
    
11. Giving specific discounts to members.
    
12. Providing assistance for placing bookings, including call centre support.
    
13. Accepting orders on a payment gateway promoted by the platform.
    
14. Collecting payments through registered electronic payment modes.
    
15. Packaging products with its own branding instead of the trademark owner’s packaging.
    
16. Transporting products to purchasers.
    
17. Employing delivery personnel for product delivery.
    
18. Accepting cash payments for product sales.
    
19. Transmitting payments to sellers after retaining commission.
    
20. Promoting affiliated companies on more favourable terms than other sellers.
    
21. Entering into favourable arrangements with various sellers.
    
22. Arranging for product exchanges in case of customer complaints.
    
23. Providing or arranging for product servicing.
    
24. Booking ad space or ad words on search engines.
    
25. Using trademarks through meta-tags or in the website’s source code to attract traffic.
    
26. Deep-linking to the trademark owner’s website.
    

The court held that if a significant number of these factors are present, a platform "could be said to cross the line from being an intermediary to an active participant" and "could be liable for infringement due to its active participation."

The court also noted:

"While safe-harbour provisions for intermediaries are meant to promote genuine businesses that are inactive intermediaries, the obligation to observe due diligence, coupled with intermediary guidelines that require hosted information to not violate IP rights, shows that e-commerce platforms that actively conspire, abet, aid, or induce unlawful acts on their websites cannot go unpunished."

However, later in Amazon vs Amway, the Delhi HC overturned this order and affirmed that Section 79 does not distinguish between "active" and "passive" intermediaries for safe-harbour eligibility.

  

“In terms of Section 79 of the IT Act, there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned. In terms of Section 79, an intermediary shall not be liable for any third-party information, data or communication link made available or posted by it, as long as it complies with Sections 79 (2) or (3) of the IT Act.” – Delhi HC (2020)
