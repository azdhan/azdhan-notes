**Karnataka says social media ban is Centre’s domain; Education dept to seek reccomendations

Despite the [Supreme Court’s recent order](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-sc-csam-regulations-intermediaries-social-media-ban/) against the blanket social media ban and age-based restrictions, the Karnataka state government said that it will seek centeral government’s recommendations on the social media ban/restrictions for children, according to the [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/karnataka-turns-to-centre-on-social-media-ban-for-under-16s-4188663) report. The uunnamed state officials had reportedly said that social media ban “comes under the Centre's domain.”

The state government had apparently realised that it can enforce the restrictions only on the devices that they have control on, not on all devices, like that of parents’. In this context, Karnataka’s Department of School Education & Literacy is likely to ask the state Cabinet next month to make recommendations, the report said. MediaNama has not independently verified the report.

 "At the state-level, we will regulate the usage of computers in schools and colleges. A complete social media ban is a larger issue on which the Centre has to take a decision. This is a subject that comes under the Centre's domain." – Anonymous govt official. 

What did the supreme court said about social media bans and restrictions: The Supreme Court's October 15 order, which Karnataka’s Additional Chief Secretary of School Education & Literacy, Rashmi V Mahesh referred to, also talked about  age-based restrictions to social media. As [MediaNama reported](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-sc-csam-regulations-intermediaries-social-media-ban/), the bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K.V. Chandran had said the following two particular points about the social media bans and restrictions: 

“...we have no hesitation in saying that the right to access and use the internet is a fundamental right of every individual under Article 21 read with Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution. Our children, irrespective of their age, enjoy the same rights, and to deny the same altogether would be as absurd as saying children below a certain age do not have a conscience worth protecting or a freedom of speech worth expressing. The safety of our children and their freedom are not at odds with each other. Our children are entitled to both.

The means by which measures relating to age-based restrictions are achieved are themselves an overreach. Such processes, inevitably, always require identifying and ascertaining the age of each and every user, continuous collection and monitoring of sensitive data and an overall disproportionate risk of misuse and surveillance, the kind which finds no place within the right to privacy of our people.” –  Supreme Court.

How Karantaka government is approaching social media ban: In the 2026-27 Budget, then-Chief Minister Siddaramaiah had said: 

"With the objective of preventing adverse effects of increasing mobile usage on children, usage of social media will be banned for children under the age of 16." - then karnataka CM

While then-CM had spoke of banning, the Information Technology Minister Priyank Kharge told Deccan Herald that the government is still talking to social media platforms and possibly exploring algorithmic-based regulations: 

"We're trying to see how we can regulate [access]...In Australia and other countries that banned social media, we've seen children bypassing the age verification step. So, now, we're trying to see how algorithms can be regulated." – Karnataka IT Minister Priyank Kharge

Additional Chief Secretary of School Education & Literacy, Rashmi V Mahesh had very narrowly referred to the Supreme Court's October 15 order, without specifically talking about the banning or restrictions.  She said that the SC judgement “aligns well with the state government's interventions” such as inclusion of computers and AI into their pedagogy.

MediaNama [reported on the announcement](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-karnataka-cm-social-media-ban-under-16/) on March 6. At that time, "neither the government nor any Member of Legislative Assembly (MLA) has introduced or presented a draft bill on the proposed social media ban in the Assembly." MediaNama's [September 29 report](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-sc-social-media-rules-minors/) noted that neither Karnataka nor Andhra Pradesh, which announced restrictions for under-13s the same day, had introduced a bill, and that Siddaramaiah resigned in May.

The age-based restrictions and the central government: Days after Karnataka's announcement, government sources said the Centre was preparing a [three-tier framework](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-india-social-media-age-restrictions-law/) for ages 8 to 12, 12 to 16 and 16 to 18 in place of a ban. On August 20, the Delhi High Court [declined to order a ban](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-delhi-hc-social-media-ban-government/), leaving the question to the government. In September, Solicitor General Tushar Mehta [told](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-sc-social-media-rules-minors/) the Supreme Court that the Centre will amend the IT Rules to stop platforms from allowing users under 18 to create accounts.

What is the difficulty in state-level social media ban implementation: In an [article](https://www.medianama.com/2026/02/223-india-social-media-ban-children/), Nikhil Pahwa, MediaNama's founder and editor had made the following point addressing the impossibility of state-level social media ban: 

“The difference between ten years ago and now is that companies do have geolocation data based on IP addresses. But geolocation is largely inaccurate. Sometimes you log in to Gmail in Delhi, and the login confirmation that hits your phone, assumes you’re in Noida or Ghaziabad. In instances where state boundaries are very close, like Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, you’re going to create a geolocation conflict. If one state bans and the other doesn’t, how exactly are platforms supposed to enforce that?

Unfortunately, the way regulators think about this is, ‘Our job is to impose a restriction, your job is to implement it, so it’s your problem.’ But it doesn’t work like that, because you’re effectively asking for something that may not be technically feasible. Feasibility should also be taken into consideration when creating law. “ – [Nilhil Pahwa](https://www.medianama.com/2026/02/223-india-social-media-ban-children/)

He also attributed the state action to the union MeitY's inaction. 

"There is a trend of states bringing out laws specific to the Internet, and this fragmentation of laws governing the Internet is problematic and because the Internet transcends physical boundaries, and it’s better if the Centre does it…. So States are trying to fill the gap left by MEITY's abdication of responsibility. The inaction from the Centre nudges the states to act. Expect more state action on Social Media, not less," he wrote.

Fixing social media algorithms and design as an alternative solution? Kharge's remark that the state is now looking at how algorithms can be regulated moves towards a question Pahwa posed in February. "The problem is that instead of regulating the system, governments are regulating access to the system," Pahwa argued against the blanket social media bans.

However, at the moment, there is no framework or policy, either at the center or at the state-level for regulating the algorithms to mitigate the risks and harms posed by the social media platforms on children. 

“The conversation should move from safety by design to safety by default. All your opt-in options that aren’t privacy-centric should be checked off by default, such as location sharing and content access for under 18s. Safety by design is a good philosophy for a new platform, but it’s time to move to safety by default,” – [a speaker at the Medianama event.](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-safety-by-design-social-media-default-online-platforms/)
