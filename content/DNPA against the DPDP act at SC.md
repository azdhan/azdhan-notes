# ‘What's the point of consent in news reporting?’ DNPA challenges DPDP Act at SC; warns of pre-censorship regime

The Supreme Court of India has refused to stay the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) without hearing the government’s side of arguments and listed the matter for November 17, 2026. This is the first hearing of Digital News Publishers Association’s (DNPA) challenge to the DPDP Act at the Supreme Court of India. 

Advocate representing DNPA, Madhavi Divan, argued the certain provisions in DPDP Act would enable a “complete regime of pre-censorship” if the media organisations and journalists have to take a prior consent from the sources or the subject of the story before publishing a news report. Under the DPDP Act, the sources or the subjects of the news articles could be considered as data principles and thereby making them eligible for the rights to edit/erasure.

DNPA Members: Digital News Publishers Association (DNPA) is a body of digital news publishers and media houses in India. These include both traditional television channels, like Gautham Adani-acquired NDTV or Mukesh Ambani-owned Network 18 as well as traditional print newspapers like The Hindu, Indian Express, Jagran Media. All of these organisations have web-based publications too. A full list of the DNPA members along with the names and designations is at the end of this article.

Here is the lightly edited transcript of Advocate Madhavi Divan’s address to the Supreme Court, who represented the news organisations’ body: 

“This is perhaps the first comprehensive petition by what we call legacy media. There has been some debate over citizen journalists. [But] We are the traditional, conventional legacy media. Would the Court consider issuing notice on the stay application? Some of these provisions are concerning… changing the whole concept of news.” 

“The concept of news has to be considered. If I am left to the mercy of the data principal as to whether he or she will give consent, how will news be reported? ... This will close down newsrooms. What is the point if I have to ask for permission and consent?” 

“When the Court examines this [DPDP] Act in detail, it will find that it was not meant for journalism at all. In fact, the [earlier versions excluded journalism](https://www.medianama.com/2025/01/223-what-is-missing-draft-data-protection-rules-2025/#:~:text=of%20exemptions%20for-,journalists,-%3A). We do not know why this has happened. Clearly, it was not meant for us.”

“We have raised the argument concerning exemptions as well. Otherwise, newsrooms will shut down if we have to ask for permission. It is a complete regime of pre-censorship. We have to ask your permission if we report on you. How can that ever be? What is news otherwise? 

“If we [news organisations] report something wrong, incorrect or inaccurate, we must face the consequences.” 

“As far as we are concerned, we straddle different forms of media, whether print or broadcast. We are covered under the [Cable Television framework](https://indiacode.gov.in/act/9d689ed7-abb8-4d14-ae6c-5540a2383d1c), [uplinking and downlinking regulations](https://mib.gov.in/sites/default/files/2024-05/uplinking-and-downlinking-guidelines-2022-english.pdf), [IT Rules](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/03/0b576f2071694b52e4cd6bb1b6dfab1e.pdf) and the [Press Council](https://www.presscouncil.nic.in/Norms.aspx). We are regulated under all these frameworks.”

What did the SC respond: Midway through Divan's address to the court, Chief Justice of India, Surya Kant, said: “You are the domain experts. You are in this field. You are the best persons to suggest to the Union, as well as to us, how you will ensure that there is no misuse.”

[Reuters reported first](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/ambani-adani-outlets-among-indian-publishers-contesting-modis-privacy-law-2026-10-26/) about the DNPA’s petition, which was originally filed on October 6. It said that it had reviewed the 698-page confidential filing by the DNPA with the Supreme Court. Here is what the Reuters report revealed:

Consultations with the Indian government: Before this petition in the Supreme Court of India, DNPA said that it had consulted with the Indian government and told the court that the publishers received “assurances” in an August meeting that the law would protect press freedom. However, despite such private assurances, the Act that was passed and the Rules that were notified doesn’t have the specific carve outs of contentious provisions.  

The August letter attached to the petition reportedly read: 

“[The Indian government] has chosen to grant broad exemptions to the state, while failing to extend any corresponding ⁠protection to ​the press". – DNPA petition. 

The petition is referring to the [provisions of the DPDP Act](https://www.medianama.com/2023/08/223-summary-india-digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023/#:~:text=What%20are%20the-,exemptions,-to%20the%20government) that exempts a few data fiduciary obligations from government and its agencies, like the data retention period and data principal’s right to edit/erasure from the record held by the government. You can read more about it [here](https://www.medianama.com/2025/01/223-dpdp-rules-2025-exemptions-govermment-overreach/).

What the news publishers are arguing: The news organisations body argues that the DPDP Act has no written exemption for journalism, unlike data protection laws in the EU, Singapore and the UK. Reuters reported that it reviewed a confidential filing by the DNPA. Some of their arguments are:

- Consent: Reuters reported that DNPA says a journalist would have to notify the subject of an investigation and seek consent. According to the filing, the petition argues that "would allow that subject to destroy evidence, intimidate witnesses," as quoted by Reuters.
    
- Erasure: DNPA argues that people can withdraw consent at any time and demand erasure with no public-interest safeguard. It says this could force members to unpublish or heavily redact factual reports, according to Reuters.
    
- Information demands: The filing reportedly argues that the government's power to call for information could extend to "privileged communications" held by a reporter or an informant/source. It also says the power could deter whistleblowers too.
    
- Blocking and fines: DNPA objects to the Data Protection Board's power to impose fines. It also objects to the government's power to block information in the "interests of the general public", per Reuters.
    

The DNPA petition seeks the quashing of these contentious provisions that would impact investigative journalism and could possibly reduce it to a passive observer of events rather than a journalistic force that scrutinizes and challenges them.

“The [DPDP Act] provisions ‘constitute pre-censorship which is anathema to any democracy worth its name’.” – DNPA petition, reviewed by Reuters.

Previous push back against DPDP Act from media bodies: 

- August 2023: The EGI said the Bill lacked journalistic exemptions. It cited the [Srikrishna Committee's warning](https://www.medianama.com/2023/08/223-data-protection-bill-no-exemption-journalistic-activities-egi/) that consent-based grounds mean accounts "unfavourable to the data principal would simply not get published". 
    
- February 2024: The EGI asked MeitY for a class exemption for journalistic processing under [Section 17(5)](https://www.medianama.com/2024/02/223-editors-urges-ministry-exempt-journalists-provisions-data-protection-act/). It said existing Press Council of India norms already set adequate standards. As MediaNama [reported](https://www.medianama.com/2024/02/223-editors-urges-ministry-exempt-journalists-provisions-data-protection-act/), Section 17 (5) of the DPDP Act allows the government to exempt a data fiduciary or a class of data fiduciaries from any provision of the DPDPA for a specified period through a notification, before the expiry of five years from the date of commencement of the Act.
    
- June 2025: The Press Club of India (PCI) and 22 other press bodies [submitted a memorandum](https://pressclubofindia.org/Memorandum-2025.pdf) to MeitY. They said they did not seek repeal of the law, only safe safeguards that protect press freedom.
    
- July 28 and August 22, 2025: MeitY Secretary S. Krishnan [met press bodies](https://www.hindustantimes.com/india-news/journalist-bodies-submit-35-faqs-to-meity-on-dpdp-act-101755963171735.html), who then submitted 35 FAQs. They also gave officials a copy of Article 85 of the GDPR as a reference model.
    
- November 2025: The Rules were notified. The PCI said its efforts "were met with a wall of obfuscation put up by the Executive", [MediaNama reported](https://www.medianama.com/2025/11/223-press-club-dpdp-rules-journalism-exemptions/). The [EGI said](https://www.medianama.com/2025/11/223-egi-journalists-indias-digital-data-protection-rules/) MeitY's July 2025 verbal assurance had not been followed by any binding clarification or amendment.
    

Before the news assocoiation’s challenge to the DPDP Act, the Supreme Court of India was reviewing a batch of petitions challenging the same DPDP Act, 2023, and the DPDP Rules, 2025, but the following petitions cover wider issues than the DNPA’s petition. 

As detailed by [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-supreme-court-dpdp-act-challenge/) and [Supreme Court Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/cases/constitutionality-of-the-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023/), the petitioners who challenged the constituionality of certain provisions are:

- Geeta Seshu and SFLC: A PIL filed via Senior Advocate Indira Jaising questioning the law's distinctions between public and private data.
    
- The Reporters' Collective and Nitin Sethi: Represented by the Internet Freedom Foundation (IFF), challenging Section 36 of the DPDP Act and Rule 23 of the DPDP Rules, 2025.
    
-  National Campaign for People's Right to Information (NCPRI) and Venkatesh Nayak: Separate petitions similarly challenging Section 36 of the DPDP Act and Rule 23 of the DPDP Rules, 2025.
    

These matters are still pending at the supreme court of India.
