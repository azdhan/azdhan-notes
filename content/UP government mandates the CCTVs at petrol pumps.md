**# Why Allahabad HC is right in questioning whether UP can force NCR petrol pumps to install CCTV surveillance

When a vehicle enters a petrol pump, Uttar Pradesh government wants the petrol pumps in the National Capital Region (NCR) to have CCTVs with Automatic Number Plate Recognition (ANPR) capabilities that would let authorities check a vehicle's registration number against a government database, according to [LiveLaw’s](https://www.livelaw.in/high-court/allahabad-high-court/allahabad-hc-mandatory-anpr-cameras-ncr-petrol-pumps-force-retailers-bear-cost-553324) report. 

In this case, the Allahabad High Court has questioned whether the UP order can force petrol and diesel retail outlet owners in NCR to bear the cost of installing ANPR-powered CCTV cameras by September 30, 2026.  

The High Court was hearing a petition by the Uttar Pradesh Petroleum Traders Association, which represents petrol and diesel retail outlet owners. A bench of Justice JJ Munir and Justice Indrajeet Shukla said a serious question arises over whether the Uttar Pradesh government's letter dated June 4, 2026, also obliges retail outlet owners to pay for the cameras. This direction was reportedly issued through the directions of the Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM).

What did UP petrol association say: The association argued that no law gives either the state government or the oil companies the authority to make owners bear the cost of the CCTV infrastructure. It said its members have no objection to ANPR cameras being installed, but they cannot be made to pay for them. It also argued that making owners bear the cost amounts to a compulsory exaction of money, in violation of Articles 300A and 265 of the Constitution, which protects against deprivation of property without authority of law bars the levy or collection of tax without authority of law. The association told the court that the authorities and oil companies were threatening coercive action because of the September 30 deadline.

The state government’s lawyers and Indian Oil Corporation Limited both sought time. Meanwhile, the court directed that "until 05.10.2026, no coercive measures shall be taken" against members of the association to make them install ANPR cameras. It clarified that if the state government installs the cameras at its own cost, the association's members would immediately facilitate the installation.

Why it matters: It is important to note that several states or municipalities or local police or administrations sometimes issue advisories or notices asking the businesses or commercial establishments to have CCTV infrastructure. 

For instance, dating back to over a decade back, in 2015, Tamil Nadu's Vellopre police [asked](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/tamil-nadu/policepublic-meet-shops-asked-to-install-cctv-cameras/article7650870.ece/amp/) the region's commercial establishments to install surveillance cameras to help them in crime detection. They also asked the CCTVs to be switched on continuously throughout the day and night. Now, coming back to 2026. A similar incident happened in [Punjab's Jalandhar](https://www.facebook.com/PoliceCommissionerateJalandhar/videos/under-the-sampark-initiative-commissionerate-police-mandates-factories-and-shops/637603631932582/), [Gujrat's Gandhinagar](https://home.gujarat.gov.in/upload/GUJRAT_ACT_2022_NOTIFICATIONGujaratPublicSafety_290722.pdf).  

Similarly, citing the environmental concerns, in June 2025 the Haryana Government also issued a similar notice. They also wanted to have ANPR-powered cameras which can "flag End-of-Life Vehicles (ELVs) through a central surveillance system,” according to TL Satya, Commissioner and Secretary to the Government, Haryana Transport Department, as reported in [The Tribune.](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/haryana/no-fuel-for-overage-vehicles-in-ncr-districts-from-nov/amp/?utm=relatedarticles)  

"These ANPR cameras will be linked to the VAHAN database and serve as part of a real-time surveillance system feeding into Command and Control Centres. Once a vehicle is identified as an ELV or found lacking a valid Pollution Under Control Certificate (PUCC), an audio alert will be triggered to notify the fuel station operator, allowing for immediate action such as denial of fuel, issuing challans or impounding." -- a Commissioner at Haryana Transport Department (June 2025)

MediaNama’s Take: Covert and overt surveillance everywhere, in real life and on digital life has been increasingly becoming a new norm with each step taken forward resembling a path towards a surveillance state. The core feature of a surveillance state is to see surveillance as a solution to any problem. (a brief list to recognise a surveillance state is at the end.) 

Yes, CCTVs can help immensely in crime investigations and any CCTV installations at the personal capacity can also be quite helpful to the public at large. However, while the larger checks & balances, purpose & access limitations around surveillance in India is another issue, mandating private businesses to install CCTVs shouldn’t be acceptable. 

If not a cost transferring measure, what is it? Especially when the state already has the surveillance infrastructure and the apparatus is already in place? Surveillance infrastructure should be paid by the state that wants it, not pushed onto businesses or the public directly. Public service/utility is the whole point of taxation, isn’t it?

If CCTV, particularly the ones with the ANPR capability, cameras at petrol pumps are a required safety feature for their businesses, it should be  their prerogative/choice. If private players are mandated to have CCTV installations, what next? CCTV cameras with facial recognition technology? Will those harddrives/servers be asked to be mandatorily linked with state-owned databases/systems?  

Nonetheless, here are a few symptoms to recognise a surveilance state, according to UK-based advocacy group [Privacy International](https://privacyinternational.org/blog/1513/defining-surveillance-state): 

- sees surveillance as the solution to complex social issues.
    
- collects information on everyone, without regard to innocence or guilt, and pretends it is not surveillance.
    
- secretly redefines laws, and the language of the law.
    
- monitors both threats to the state's interests and threats to its surveillance practices.
    
- conducts itself under veils of secrecy, and creates monsters of those who wish to debate it, understand it, or inform it.
    
- avoids democratic and judicial authorisation and scrutiny.
    
- deputises the private sector, by compelling access to companies' data stores, then paying industry to run its control systems, and even selling our information to industry as though it belongs to the state.
    
- is incompetent and relies upon technologies and data with inherent limitations.
    
- wrongly defines criminals and terrorists with little right to redress, discriminates and excludes.
    
- undermines personal and economic security and all other social objectives to accomplish its goal: whatever is in the state's surveillance interests. As such, it sees only the balance between its needs and our selfish individual wants.
    
- cannot be questioned because it has the monopoly on knowledge, and cannot be overseen because it can control what can be disclosed.
    
- will defend itself with rhetoric of fear, and then its powers are used for purposes well beyond their origin and justification.
    
- plays a long game, irrespective of who or what party is in power it seeks its own policies.
