**# CBFC data breach exposed movie scripts, censor cuts summaries and distribution deals of 500+ films: Report

A data breach at the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC) exposed documents from applications for more than 500 titles, including dozens of feature films, reported  [The Hindu’s Aroon Deep](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/technology/hundreds-of-applications-exposed-in-censor-board-data-breach/article71619870.ece) on October 25, 2026. The leaked data included sensitive film distribution redacted and unredacted agreements; censor cuts mandated by CBFC and film scripts too. 

The leak appears to have originated from the e-Cinepramaan portal, which filmmakers use to submit applications for certification. In India, CBFC’s film certifications are mandatory for all theatrical releases. The records date from December 2025 to August 2026. According to The Hindu, the documents were packaged as datasets and uploaded to the Internet Archive, where they could be freely downloaded for days. Most were posted earlier this month, and all were taken down on October 12. The uploader’s display name reportedly was a Russian rendering of the name of former CBFC chairman Prasoon Joshi, and the Internet Archive account has since been deleted. However, MediaNama has not independently reviewed the leaked documents.

What was leaked? In its [explainer on the leak](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/technology/what-was-leaked-in-the-censor-board-data-breach-explained/article71625769.ece), The Hindu said the leaked data contained:

- Shooting scripts: The reported leak had detailed shot-by-shot translations and annotations. One script reportedly carried labels on swear words and clarifications on dialogue.
    
- Summaries of cuts mandted by the CBFC: The Hindu notes this is supposed to be public information under Section 5A(2) of the Cinematograph Act, 1952, but the board had put such data behind an “under maintenance” message on its website.
    
- Film distribution agreements were exposed: The publication noted that the film distribution licenses for the international films distributed in India and for Indian films licensed for dubbed versions in other languages were publicly exposed.  
      
    These include local distribution agreements for films such as: Brazil’s Oscar-nominated The Secret Agent and the Hollywood film Good Luck, Have Fun, Don’t Die. At least three agreements revealed the fees paid to license the films, either unredacted or improperly redacted, as per the documents reviewed by the publication.   
    

What is CBFC’s response after the breach: While Interent Archive told The Hindu that the user had deleted the account, according to a few excerpts from The HIndu’s report, this is what CBFC has said:

 “CBFC has reviewed the information shared regarding alleged unauthorised access to CBFC’s eCinePramaan Portal…However, based on the information examined so far, no trace has been found of the certification-related documents that were claimed to have been accessed.”

“As a matter of caution, a complaint has been filed with cybercrime authorities, and the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team [CERT-In] has also been notified of the allegations and provided with the information shared.”

“The technical team has also conducted a thorough analysis of the servers and databases to assess whether any external attacks may have compromised their security. The findings of this analysis are being considered alongside the ongoing forensic examination.”

It is telling that CBFC said that it has found ‘no trace of the document’, yet it has filed a complaint with cybercrime authorities, notified CERT-In and is conducting a forensic examination as “a matter of caution”.

It is a recurring pattern that the Indian orgnanisations initially either deny the breach/hack, or voluntarily downplay the attack by minimising the severity of the attack, as happened with [MietY’s response](https://x.com/GoI_MeitY/status/2112914708261179404?s=20) with [eMudhra’s weak private keys that are used to certify the website](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-emudhra-security-flaw-weak-private-keys-explained/)s and State Bank of India’s response to data breach of over three lakh consumers, which termed it as [“very very small.”](https://x.com/TheOfficialSBI/status/2112856995841916979?s=20) Or, sometimes, the data found on dark web mismatches with the leak accepted by the organisation, as happened with the [Dodo Payments case](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-dark-web-dodo-data-breach/). The CBFC’s response, that it has found no trace of the documents yet carrying the so-called forensic examination too fits this pattern of denial and downplaying.

A brief list of a few breaches in India: 

- October 2026: SBI confirmed a suspected breach involving [3,897 customer records](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-sbi-data-breach-3897-customers-dark-web/) that appeared on the dark web, but has been silent on whether affected customers were informed.
    
- September 2026: Dodo Payments said a breach of its internal analytics system did not expose credentials, but [a dark web listing reviewed by MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-dark-web-dodo-data-breach/) claims the stolen data includes API keys, KYC records and customer data.
    
- August 2026: HCLTech said it found [no evidence of a breach of its systems](https://www.medianama.com/2026/08/223-hcltech-no-evidence-system-breach/) and that the leaked employee data may be limited and years old.
    
- July 2026: Bank of Baroda began a [forensic probe](https://www.medianama.com/2026/07/223-bank-of-baroda-forensic-probe-customer-data-dark-web/) after customer data appeared on the dark web. MediaNama also [found files linked to the Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant project](https://www.medianama.com/2026/07/223-reliance-data-breach-kudankulam-files-dark-web/) in leaked Reliance data on a ransomware site, and researchers found [security flaws in UMANG](https://www.medianama.com/2026/07/223-researchers-find-security-flaws-umang-exposed-user-data-across-government-services/) that allegedly exposed EPFO, LPG and Aadhaar-linked data.
    
- June 2026: Bajaj Auto confirmed a [ransomware attack](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-bajaj-auto-systems-hit-by-ransomware/) on its systems and technology subsidiary, and Ultrahuman [notified users more than two months](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-ultrahuman-suffers-security-breach-exposing-users-wellness-data/) after a March 27 attack.
    
- May 2026: A student researcher [demonstrated access to CBSE’s On-Screen Marking portal](https://www.medianama.com/2026/05/223-cbse-hacked-cybersecurity-researcher-demonstrates-access-official-denials/) after officials denied his earlier findings, and HDFC AMC [reported a cybersecurity incident](https://www.medianama.com/2026/05/223-hdfc-amc-notifies-cybersecurity-incident-it-infrastructure-unlikely-affect-business/) to the stock exchanges.
    

The CBFC and the online censorship: To be clear, CBFC has no jurisdiction over the online content, including films and series. However, while the [censorship by CBFC has its own problems](https://article-14.com/post/how-a-modi-era-ecosystem-of-official-unofficial-censorship-is-transforming-india-s-film-industries-679fdbcd6f914), the censorship generally happening in India is also impacting the online content too.

The case of Punjab ’95 (retitled Satluj for its very brief streaming release) shows how far the CBFC’s influence could be over-reaching OTT space. The film, about human-rights activist Jaswant Singh Khalra, was [reportedly held up by the board for over three years,](https://www.medianama.com/2026/07/223-zee5s-removal-punjab-95-satluj-mysterious-unofficial-censorship-indian-ott/) which sought over 127 cuts. When it was released on Zee5, it was pulled in India within around 48 hours. However, there is no official notice of if the Ministry of Infomation and Broadcasting (MIB) or Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) or CBFC has sent notices or asked Zee5 to remove it from its OTT platform. 

In an attempt to push a CBFC-model of age-graded content classification and censorship is also being discussed. In September, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) [reportedly](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-rss-ott-regulation/) formed an informal group to propose OTT regulation, with a source saying the group wants “something like a censor board” for the online streaming industry.
